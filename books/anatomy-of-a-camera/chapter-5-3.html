Autofocus Systems: Phase vs. Contrast Detection

Part V of XVI
Category Technical
Sheet 029 / 089
Depends On 5.2
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapters 5.1 and 5.2 described focus and depth of field as facts of optics — a plane of convergence, a zone of acceptable blur around it. Neither chapter said anything about how a camera actually finds that plane without a human turning a ring by feel. That's a measurement problem, and it has two genuinely different solutions built into modern cameras, arrived at decades apart and still both in use today: phase detection and contrast detection.

The Question an Autofocus System Has to Answer

Every autofocus system, regardless of mechanism, is trying to answer the same question: is the current plane of focus in front of the subject, behind it, or exactly on it, and if it's wrong, which direction and how far does the lens need to move to fix it? The two approaches differ entirely in how they extract that answer from the light hitting the camera, and that difference in method produces real, practical differences in speed and behavior.

Phase Detection: Comparing Two Halves of the Same Light

Phase detection works by splitting incoming light into two separate paths, sampled from opposite edges of the lens's aperture opening, and comparing the two resulting images directly. When the plane of focus sits exactly at the subject, both split images align perfectly. When it doesn't, the two images shift apart from each other, and critically, the direction and size of that shift directly indicates which way the lens is wrong and by roughly how much — the system doesn't need to guess and check, it can calculate the correction in a single measurement. This is what makes phase detection fast: one comparison, one confident move, largely regardless of how far off the initial focus happened to be.

Contrast Detection: Hunting for the Sharpest Signal

Contrast detection takes a different approach entirely, one that doesn't compare two images at all. Instead, it relies on a simple, observable fact: a scene that's in true focus has more contrast at fine edges — sharper transitions between light and dark — than the same scene slightly out of focus. The system moves the lens, measures contrast at each position, and looks for the point where contrast peaks. Because it has no way to know in advance which direction improves contrast, it typically has to move the lens one way, check whether contrast rose or fell, and adjust — an iterative search rather than a single calculated correction.

Phase detection measures which way and how far in one step. Contrast detection has to search for the answer by trial and error, checking whether each move helped before committing to the next one.

Why This Difference Actually Matters in Practice

The practical consequence follows directly from the mechanism. Phase detection tends to lock onto a subject quickly and predictably, including subjects that are moving, because a single measurement already contains direction and distance information. Contrast detection tends to be slower and can visibly "hunt" — the lens moving past the point of best focus, detecting the overshoot by a drop in contrast, and correcting back — because it has no way to know it has already passed the peak until it measures the fall on the other side. Contrast detection's advantage is precision at the point it does converge on, since it's measuring focus directly rather than inferring it from a comparison, and it doesn't require dedicated phase-detection hardware built into the sensor or a separate sensor module. Most modern cameras don't force a choice between the two; they run both systems together, using phase detection for the fast initial correction and contrast detection to fine-tune the last small step, an approach usually described as hybrid autofocus.

Understanding both mechanisms explains a great deal of real-world autofocus behavior — why some cameras hunt visibly in low light or low-contrast scenes, and why continuous autofocus on moving subjects depends so heavily on phase detection's ability to predict direction rather than just detect it. Chapter 5.4 builds on this by covering the modes that decide when and how these mechanisms actually get triggered during a shoot.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Phase detection compares two split images sampled from opposite edges of the aperture, calculating direction and distance in a single measurement.
  • Contrast detection searches for the lens position that maximizes edge contrast, an iterative process with no built-in sense of direction.
  • Phase detection is generally faster and better suited to moving subjects; contrast detection is precise but prone to visible hunting.
  • Most modern cameras combine both as hybrid autofocus — phase detection for the fast initial move, contrast detection for the final fine-tune.
