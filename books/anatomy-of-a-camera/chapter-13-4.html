Chapter Thirteen, Section Four

Backing Up a Shoot Properly

Part XIII of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 074 / 089
Depends On 13.3
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 13.2 closed on a boundary: a memory card's job is to survive from shutter press to the first real copy, and nothing past that point is the card's responsibility. This chapter is about that first real copy, and the ones that should follow it. A shoot that exists in exactly one place, on exactly one piece of hardware, is not backed up — it's merely stored, and storage and backup are different guarantees against different failures.

What Backup Is Actually Insuring Against

It's worth being precise about the specific failures a backup exists to survive, because the right structure follows directly from the threat. A drive can fail mechanically or electronically, at any age and with little warning. A file can be corrupted silently, its problem invisible until the moment it's opened for real. A location — a bag, a car, an office — can be lost, stolen, or damaged wholesale, taking everything in it at once regardless of how many drives were inside. Chapter 13.2's mirrored cards already solve the narrowest version of this problem during the shoot itself; backup is the same logic extended past the point where the shoot ends and the cards are no longer the only copies that matter.

The Rule Worth Actually Following

The backup convention that has survived contact with real working photographers, repeated so often it's become closer to folklore than formal advice, is the three-two-one rule: three total copies of the material, on two different types of storage media, with one of those copies kept somewhere physically separate from the others. Each clause closes a specific gap the others leave open. Three copies means a single failure never leaves you at zero. Two different media types means a flaw specific to one storage technology — a bad batch of drives from one manufacturer, a firmware bug affecting one card generation — can't take out every copy at once. One copy offsite means a fire, theft, or flood at one location can't destroy everything, because not everything was ever in that location to begin with.

A backup that lives one room away from the original protects against drive failure and nothing else. The one-offsite clause is the only part of the rule built to survive the failures that take out an entire location, not just a single piece of hardware.

RAW Files Are the Priority, Not the Edited Export

Chapter 12.1 established why RAW files require processing rather than arriving finished, and that same asymmetry determines backup priority: an edited export can, in principle, be recreated from the RAW and the same set of adjustments; a lost RAW file cannot be recreated from anything, because it was the unrepeatable capture of a moment that no longer exists to be photographed again. Working photographers who trim what they back up to save space nearly always trim in the wrong direction, keeping polished exports and letting RAW archives lapse — exactly backward from where the actual, irreplaceable risk sits.

Verification Is Not Optional

A copy operation that reports success has not necessarily produced a usable copy — file corruption during transfer is uncommon but not rare enough to ignore, especially across long transfers or aging cards. Verified copying, which checksums source and destination and confirms they match rather than simply confirming that a copy operation completed without an error message, is worth the small amount of extra time it costs on every shoot where the material genuinely can't be recreated. This is the same principle behind Chapter 13.3's preventive posture toward sensor cleaning: the version of a task done properly before anything has gone wrong is always cheaper than the version attempted after a client has already asked where their gallery is.

Backup Discipline Scales Down, Not Just Up

None of this requires enterprise infrastructure to practice honestly. A working, affordable version of three-two-one looks like: cards copied to a laptop drive and a separate portable drive on location, with a third copy synced to cloud storage or a drive kept at a different address once back from the shoot — three copies, two media types, one location genuinely separate from the rest. Chapter 14.4 covers what happens once that archive needs to actually reach a client as a finished gallery, but everything in that later chapter assumes the material survived to be delivered at all, which is precisely what this one is built to guarantee.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Storage and backup are different guarantees — one copy in one place survives nothing but the absence of any problem at all.
  • Three-two-one — three copies, two media types, one offsite — closes drive failure, media-specific flaws, and whole-location loss.
  • RAW files are the irreplaceable priority; exports can be rebuilt from them, so trimming an archive should never start there.
  • Verified, checksummed copying confirms a backup is usable — a completed copy operation alone does not guarantee that.
