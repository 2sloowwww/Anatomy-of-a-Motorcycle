Chapter Five, Section Five

Suspension Travel

Part V of XVI
Category Engineering
Sheet 049 / 150
Depends On Ch. 5.4
Read Time ~7 Min

An adventure bike built to cross rocks, ruts, and sudden drops might offer well over 200 millimetres of suspension travel at each end. A sportbike built to carve smooth tarmac often gets by with barely half that. Neither number is simply better than the other — travel is the total workspace everything in this part of the book has been operating inside, and how much of it a given motorcycle gets is a direct trade against something Chapter 4.4 already taught you to care about.

Chapter 5.4 measured sag as a fraction of the suspension's total available travel without asking where that total figure actually comes from. This chapter asks exactly that.

Travel Is the Workspace, Not a Free Variable

Suspension travel is simply the total distance the suspension can move, from fully extended to fully compressed — the entire range within which the spring, damping, and sag this part has already covered all have to operate. A motorcycle built for genuinely rough, unpredictable terrain needs generous travel specifically because the size and suddenness of the impacts it faces — rocks, ruts, sharp-edged drops — demand far more room to absorb a hit without bottoming out than a smooth road surface ever will. A motorcycle built purely for pavement doesn't face impacts anywhere near that severe, and doesn't need anywhere near that much travel to avoid bottoming out under normal use.

The Real Cost of More Travel: Changing Geometry

Here's the trade-off that connects directly back to Chapter 4.4's rake and trail. The steering head angle and fork offset that chapter described as producing trail were described at a specific point in the suspension's stroke — effectively, at a specific ride height. As the suspension compresses and extends through its travel, that ride height changes, and with it, the steering head angle and trail change slightly too, dynamically, throughout every bump and every corner. The more total travel a suspension has, the larger that dynamic swing in geometry becomes across its full range of motion, which means a long-travel suspension is constantly asking the front end to steer with somewhat different trail than the number stamped on a spec sheet describes at rest.

A shorter-travel suspension keeps that swing much smaller, holding geometry closer to constant throughout the stroke — which is exactly why sportbikes, built around the precise, predictable steering feel Chapter 4.4 described trail producing, favour shorter travel deliberately, not as a limitation but as a way of protecting the geometry consistency that precision depends on. A long-travel adventure or off-road suspension accepts more geometry variation as the acceptable cost of having enough room to absorb terrain a shorter-travel bike would bottom out on immediately.

Suspension travel isn't just "how big a hit can this bike absorb." More travel also means more geometry change throughout the stroke — the same rake and trail Chapter 4.4 explained don't hold perfectly steady on a bike built to move through 250 millimetres of suspension rather than 120.

A Practical Consequence: Seat Height

More travel also has to physically fit somewhere, which generally means a taller suspension unit and, as a direct consequence, a taller seat height — a practical ergonomic trade-off Part XV will return to when it covers choosing a motorcycle that actually fits a given rider. This is one more example of a single spec, travel, rippling outward into consequences well beyond the suspension itself.

A Common Misconception, Cleared Up Early

By now, this book has made the same basic point often enough that it's worth stating briefly rather than at length: more of a given spec is not simply better, and travel is no exception. An adventure bike's generous travel would make a sportbike feel vague and inconsistent through a corner, its geometry swinging further than precise tarmac handling can tolerate. A sportbike's short travel bolted onto an adventure bike would bottom out constantly on exactly the terrain that bike is built to cross. Travel, like almost everything else covered in this book, is a spec chosen for a specific job, not a number to maximize.

Where This Leads

This chapter explained how much room a suspension has to work with. It hasn't yet explained how the front suspension unit is actually built to provide that room — the fork itself, and the different physical designs manufacturers use. Chapter 5.6 turns there directly.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Suspension travel is the total distance available between full extension and full compression — the workspace within which spring rate, damping, and sag all operate.
  • More travel exists specifically to absorb larger, more severe terrain impacts without bottoming out, which is why off-road and adventure motorcycles run significantly more travel than road-focused bikes.
  • More travel also means more dynamic change in steering head angle and trail throughout the stroke, since Chapter 4.4's geometry is only fixed at one specific point in that travel — shorter travel protects steering consistency, longer travel trades some of that consistency for terrain capability.
  • Suspension travel directly affects seat height, a practical ergonomic consequence Part XV will return to when covering how to choose a motorcycle that fits a given rider.
  • Travel is matched to intended use, not maximized — more travel is a genuine cost as often as it's a genuine benefit, depending entirely on what the motorcycle is actually built to do.
