LUTs, Color Space, and Delivering the Right Look

Chapter 1.4 explained why footage looks flat on import and deferred the hands-on side of color management to this part, once you had nodes and scopes to make it concrete. You now have both, and Chapter 9.6 just spent a full chapter on the specific, careful work of matching shots to each other. None of that work survives automatically. This chapter closes the loop Chapter 1.4 opened: what a LUT actually is, and how to make sure the color space a finished grade gets delivered in doesn't quietly undo everything Chapter 9.6 just built.

Color Management, Revisited With Real Vocabulary

Chapter 1.4 described color management as a translation step sitting between what a camera recorded and what your eyes are looking at, without walking through the mechanics of it — that was deliberately left for here. In practice, that translation is a pair of transforms: an input transform that converts a camera's native log or raw encoding into a working color space, and an output transform that converts the finished, graded image into whatever a display or delivery target expects. Resolve Color Management handles both automatically, project-wide, once configured; everything this part has done since Chapter 9.2 has been happening inside that already-translated middle space, which is precisely why the flat log image Chapter 1.4 described never showed up again once management was set up correctly.

What a LUT Actually Is

A LUT — a lookup table — is a fixed mapping from input color values to output color values, applied uniformly across an entire image. LUTs come in two practically distinct roles that share a name. A technical LUT does part of the color management job just described: converting a camera's specific log encoding into a normal-looking image, a mechanical translation with no creative intent behind it. A creative LUT is different in purpose entirely — a packaged, stylized look, built by a colorist and applied as a strong starting point for a scene's overall tone, the kind of thing often labeled with a mood or a genre reference.

Why a LUT Is a Start, Not a Finish

A creative LUT is tempting precisely because it's fast — one click and a project can suddenly look considerably more finished than it did a moment before. That speed is also its limit. A LUT applies the exact same mathematical shift to every shot it's dropped onto, with no awareness of which shot is under-exposed, which one already carries a color cast, or which one was shot on a different camera entirely. It's Chapter 9.6's matching problem all over again, just automated in the wrong direction: a LUT can make already-matched, already-corrected shots share a consistent creative tone efficiently, but it cannot substitute for the matching and primary correction work that has to happen first. Apply a LUT to unmatched footage and you get a set of shots that are now uniformly wrong in a new, more stylish way.

A LUT is a fast way to apply a consistent look to shots that already agree with each other. It has never been a fast way to make shots that don't agree with each other agree — that's still Chapter 9.6's job, done first.

Color Space at Delivery

The final place this whole chain can quietly break is at the very end: rendering a finished grade into an output color space that doesn't match what the delivery destination actually expects. A grade built and evaluated correctly throughout this part, in a properly managed working space, can still come out wrong on a viewer's screen if the render's output color space is mismatched with its destination — a standard streaming or broadcast delivery generally expecting Rec.709, versus an HDR delivery target expecting something considerably wider. This isn't a creative decision to make casually at the last minute; it's a technical setting that has to match the destination precisely, or every match and correction decision made throughout this part effectively gets re-translated incorrectly on the way out.

Confirming the Chain Before You Render

The practical habit worth carrying out of this chapter is a short, specific check performed before any final render: confirm the project's color management is still configured the way it was when the grade was built, confirm any LUT applied is a deliberate creative choice sitting on top of already-matched footage rather than a substitute for matching it, and confirm the output color space actually matches where the file is going. None of these three checks is difficult on its own. Skipping any one of them is the single most common way a carefully built grade ships looking like something else entirely. Chapter 9.8 closes this part by walking that full chain, start to finish, on one project.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Color management is an input transform and an output transform working together — everything graded in this part has happened inside that already-translated middle space.
  • A LUT is a fixed, uniform mapping of color values — technical LUTs handle translation, creative LUTs package a stylized look.
  • A creative LUT applies a consistent tone to shots that already match — it cannot substitute for the matching and correction work covered earlier in this part.
  • A mismatched output color space at render time can re-translate a correctly built grade incorrectly, undoing the entire chain of decisions before it.
