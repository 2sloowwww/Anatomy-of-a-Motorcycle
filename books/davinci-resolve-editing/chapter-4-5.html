Chapter Four, Section Five

Markers and Timeline Navigation

Part IV of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 024 / 095
Depends On 4.1
Read Time ~7 Min

A ten-clip rough assembly is trivial to navigate by eye. A ninety-minute documentary timeline with hundreds of clips is not, and scrubbing manually to find a specific point becomes a genuine time cost once a sequence reaches real length. This chapter covers the two tools built specifically to solve that: markers, for flagging meaningful points, and navigation shortcuts, for moving directly between them and between edit points without ever needing to eyeball the timeline and guess.

Markers: Flags You Place Deliberately

A marker is a small, timestamped flag you place at a specific point on the timeline, optionally with a color, a name, and a longer note attached. Unlike an edit point, which exists because of where one clip ends and another begins, a marker exists purely because you decided that moment was worth flagging — a place to revisit, a problem to fix, a beat that needs music, a point a collaborator asked about. Markers carry no structural meaning to the sequence itself; they're purely a layer of annotation sitting on top of it.

Color is worth using deliberately rather than defaulting to whatever color happens to be selected. A common, practical convention: one color for "needs a fix," another for "needs client or director feedback," another for "sync point to double check," and so on — a small, consistent vocabulary, similar in spirit to the flag conventions Chapter 2.4 recommended for footage review. The specific colors matter less than the consistency of what each one means across a project.

An edit point exists because of what's on the timeline. A marker exists because of what you noticed about it. They're both points in time, but only one of them is actually part of the sequence's structure.

Markers as a Communication Tool, Not Just a Personal Reminder

On a collaborative project, markers do double duty as a lightweight communication channel. A note left on a marker — "picture lock needed by Friday," "check this line reads clearly," "temp music, needs replacing" — travels with the timeline itself, visible to anyone else opening the same project, rather than living in a separate email thread or chat message disconnected from the actual point in the cut it refers to. Part XII's collaboration chapters return to this idea in the context of full team review workflows; even working solo, though, markers left for your own future self during a first pass are worth the small effort, since a note written in the moment is almost always more useful than trying to remember, hours or days later, what specifically needed attention at a given point.

Navigating Between Edit Points

Beyond markers, Resolve provides direct navigation shortcuts for jumping the playhead to the next or previous edit point on the timeline — the boundary between one clip and the next, as Chapter 4.1 defined it — without needing to scrub or click precisely. This is a genuinely fast way to move through a sequence clip by clip, and it becomes second nature quickly enough that it's worth learning early rather than defaulting to manual scrubbing out of habit. Chapter 5.2's coverage of JKL playback control builds directly on this same instinct — fast, keyboard-driven movement through a timeline rather than mouse-driven scrubbing — and treating edit-point navigation as a first small step in that direction now will make Part V's material land more naturally later.

Navigating Between Markers

Just as you can jump directly between edit points, you can jump directly between markers, independent of where clips happen to start and end. This is the more useful navigation mode specifically during a review or fix pass: rather than scrubbing through an entire sequence looking for problems, you flag every issue with a marker during one focused viewing, then return afterward and jump marker to marker, addressing each one in turn without needing to remember where they all were or re-watch material that didn't need attention.

A Habit Worth Building Early

The practical value of this chapter isn't any single feature — it's the shift from "find things on the timeline by looking" to "find things on the timeline by jumping directly to them." That shift matters more as a project grows, and it's far easier to build the habit early, on a smaller project where the difference feels optional, than to retrofit it later on a large one where manual scrubbing has quietly become the default and genuinely expensive way you navigate.

With placement, tools, and navigation all covered, Chapter 4.6 turns to a different kind of organization: linking, grouping, and compound clips — the tools for treating multiple related clips as a single, coordinated unit rather than managing each one independently.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Markers are a layer of deliberate annotation, separate from the timeline's actual structure of clips and edit points.
  • A consistent color convention for markers — problem, feedback needed, sync check — makes them useful at a glance across a whole project.
  • Markers double as a communication tool on collaborative projects, keeping notes attached to the exact point in the cut they refer to.
  • Jumping directly between edit points and between markers replaces manual scrubbing — a habit worth building before a project grows large enough to need it.
