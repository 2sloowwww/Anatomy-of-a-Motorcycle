Camera Formats and Codecs, Explained for Editors

Part II of XVI
Category Technical
Sheet 012 / 095
Depends On 2.5
Read Time ~9 Min

This isn't a chapter for camera engineers, and it isn't trying to make you one. It's the small, practical slice of codec knowledge an editor actually needs — enough to look at an unfamiliar file, recognize what kind of demands it's likely to make, and make an informed call about proxies, storage, and handling, rather than treating every format as an interchangeable mystery box that either "plays smoothly" or "doesn't," for reasons you can't predict in advance.

Codec and Container Are Two Different Things

It's worth separating two words that get used almost interchangeably in casual conversation but mean genuinely different things. A container (like MOV or MXF) is a wrapper format — it defines how video, audio, and metadata are packaged together into a single file, without dictating how the video itself is actually compressed. A codec (like ProRes, DNxHD, or H.265) is the actual method used to compress and decompress the video data inside that wrapper. Two files can share the same container and use completely different codecs, or share the same codec while wrapped in different containers. When Resolve struggles with a file, the meaningful question is almost always about the codec, not the container.

Intra-Frame Versus Long-GOP: The Distinction That Matters Most for Editing

Of everything in this chapter, this is the one distinction most worth internalizing, because it explains more real-world playback behavior than any other single fact about codecs. An intra-frame codec — ProRes and DNx families are the common examples — compresses every single frame independently, as a complete image. Decoding any given frame only requires that frame's own data. A long-GOP codec — the family most consumer and prosumer cameras record in, including most H.264 and H.265 footage — only stores certain frames in full, and represents the frames between them as differences from their neighbors, in a structure called a Group of Pictures.

Long-GOP compresses beautifully and looks great on delivery. Intra-frame scrubs beautifully and edits beautifully. Cameras optimize for the first job. Editing needs the second.

Long-GOP is excellent at achieving small file sizes for good visual quality, which is exactly why cameras favor it for recording — storage is limited and expensive on set. But it's genuinely harder to decode quickly for arbitrary, non-sequential playback, because reconstructing an arbitrary frame in the middle of a Group of Pictures may require decoding several neighboring frames first, rather than that one frame alone. This is a large part of why editing directly on long-GOP camera originals often feels sluggish, while an intra-frame proxy of the exact same footage scrubs smoothly — it's not a quality difference you're feeling, it's a decode-complexity difference, and it's precisely the problem Chapter 2.5's proxies exist to solve.

RAW: A Different Kind of Heavy

RAW formats are a separate category worth understanding on their own terms. A RAW file stores something closer to the sensor's original data before many of the processing decisions — full color processing and debayering among them — have been permanently applied, which is what gives RAW footage its wide latitude for grading changes later, a topic Part IX returns to directly. That flexibility comes at a real computational cost: playing RAW footage means Resolve is doing meaningfully more processing work in real time than playing an already-processed file of similar visual quality, which is why RAW formats are often the strongest candidates for optimized media specifically, rather than a simple proxy.

Bit Depth and Chroma Subsampling, Briefly

Two more terms worth recognizing, even without needing to master the underlying math. Bit depth describes how many distinct values a file can represent per color channel — an 8-bit file has meaningfully less tonal precision available than a 10-bit or 12-bit file, which matters directly for how much a color grade can push an image before banding or artifacts start to show, a concern Part IX will make concrete. Chroma subsampling describes how much color detail, relative to brightness detail, a codec actually stores — footage recorded at 4:2:0 stores less color information than 4:2:2 or 4:4:4, which becomes especially relevant for effects work requiring precise color isolation, like the keying techniques covered in Part X.

Neither of these needs to become an obsession for everyday editing. But both are useful context for a specific, recurring situation: understanding why footage that looks perfectly fine as delivered sometimes pushes back, visibly, once it's graded hard or composited — the file was never given enough precision, in bit depth or chroma detail, to support that particular kind of manipulation cleanly.

Turning This Into a Practical Habit

None of this needs to slow down day-to-day editing. The useful habit is a quick mental check on unfamiliar footage before a project gets underway: is this long-GOP or intra-frame — meaning, should I expect scrubbing to be sluggish, and is a proxy worth generating up front? Is this RAW — meaning, is optimized media likely the better tool, and is there real grading latitude here worth protecting later? A few seconds spent recognizing the answer, before hours of editing begin, is the entire practical payoff of this chapter.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Container and codec are different layers — the container packages the file, the codec compresses the video inside it, and playback trouble is almost always a codec issue.
  • Long-GOP footage compresses efficiently but is harder to scrub in real time; intra-frame footage is heavier on disk but far smoother to edit — this is why proxies exist.
  • RAW footage offers wide grading latitude at real computational cost, making it a strong candidate for optimized media rather than a simple proxy.
  • Bit depth and chroma subsampling determine how much a file can be graded or composited before its limits become visible — worth a glance before heavy manipulation.
