Chapter Two, Section Three

Importing and Organizing Footage

Part II of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 009 / 095
Depends On 2.2
Read Time ~8 Min

Importing footage into Resolve is mechanically simple — drag files in, or point the Media Storage browser at a folder, and clips appear in your Media Pool within seconds. The part that actually determines whether a project stays manageable isn't the import itself. It's the handful of decisions you make in that same first hour: how footage gets organized on arrival, whether it gets backed up before anything else happens to it, and how closely your bin structure should mirror the folder structure your camera or DIT handed you. Get these right at the start of a project, and organization mostly takes care of itself from there. Get them wrong, and you're reorganizing hundreds of clips two weeks in, under deadline pressure.

The Media Storage Browser Versus Simple Drag-and-Drop

Resolve gives you two everyday ways to bring footage into a project. Simple drag-and-drop — pulling files or folders from your operating system's file browser directly into the Media Pool — works fine for quick, small imports. The Media Storage browser, a panel built into the Media page specifically for browsing connected drives and cards, is the better habit for anything larger, because it lets you preview clips, select specific ranges, and bring footage in without needing a separate file browser window open alongside Resolve at all. Chapter 3.2's discussion of Source Tape on the Cut page builds on the same underlying browsing tools introduced here.

Backup Before Organization, Not After

Before any organizing decision, there's a more important one: has this footage been safely backed up somewhere other than the single card or drive it was shot on? This isn't strictly a Resolve feature — it's a production discipline — but it belongs in this chapter because it's the step editors skip when they're eager to start cutting, and it's the step that causes genuine disaster when a single drive fails. Many editors use Resolve's Clone Tool, available from the Media page, specifically for this: verified, checksum-confirmed copies of camera media to two separate destinations before that original card gets formatted and reused. Treat this as step zero, before step one of organizing anything.

An import that skips verification and lands on a single, unbacked-up drive isn't actually finished — it's one drive failure away from a production having never happened at all.

Preserve the Source Structure, Then Layer Your Own On Top

Camera media typically arrives with its own folder structure — often organized by card, by clip number, or by a camera-generated naming scheme. It's tempting to immediately reorganize everything into a scheme that makes more sense to you. Resist that urge for the raw files themselves: preserve the original folder structure on disk exactly as it came off the camera or from your DIT, because that structure is often meaningful (it may encode camera, card, or roll information you'd otherwise have to reconstruct by hand) and because reshuffling original files on disk after the fact is exactly the kind of change that can silently break other tools or backups relying on the original paths.

Instead, build your actual organization inside the Media Pool, using bins, as Chapter 2.2 covered. A common, durable structure is a top-level bin per shoot day, with sub-bins per camera or per scene beneath it — mirroring how the footage was actually captured, which tends to match how you'll think about it later when you're looking for a specific shot. The underlying files on disk can stay in their native structure; your bins are a separate, flexible layer of organization on top, exactly because bins are views rather than physical locations, as the previous chapter established.

Naming Conventions Worth Deciding Early

Camera-generated filenames are rarely meaningful to a human at a glance — a string like `A004_C012_0612AB` tells you nothing about content without additional context. You don't need to rename every source file (and for the reasons above, often shouldn't), but deciding early on a consistent naming convention for the things you do control — bin names, subclips, compound clips, and timelines you create — pays off constantly. A simple, predictable scheme (day, scene, and a short description) beats a clever one you won't remember the logic of three weeks later.

What Happens When Media Moves

Even with careful organization, footage sometimes needs to move — consolidated onto a bigger drive, migrated to shared storage, archived off a laptop before a new job starts. When that happens, Resolve's references (Chapter 2.1's pointer model again) can no longer find the files at their old path, and clips show as offline in the Media Pool. This isn't data loss — it's a broken pointer, and Resolve's relink tools let you point the project at the new location, usually resolving every affected clip at once if the folder structure was moved intact rather than reshuffled. Part XV covers relinking and conform issues in more depth for the cases that don't resolve cleanly; the practical takeaway here is simpler: moving media isn't dangerous, but it isn't invisible either, and it's worth doing deliberately rather than by accident.

Setting Up for What Comes Next

With footage imported, backed up, and organized into a bin structure that reflects how it was shot, the project is in a genuinely good starting position. The next two chapters build directly on this foundation: Chapter 2.4 covers the metadata and flagging system that turns a merely organized Media Pool into a searchable one, and Chapter 2.5 covers proxies — the practical answer to footage that's technically well-organized but too heavy to scrub smoothly on the hardware you're editing with.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Verified backups to at least two destinations come before organizing anything — treat it as step zero on every new shoot's footage.
  • Preserve camera-original folder structures on disk; build your real organization as a separate layer of bins inside the Media Pool instead.
  • A simple, consistent naming convention for bins, subclips, and timelines matters more over time than a clever one-off scheme.
  • Moving media breaks the project's pointers to it, not the data itself — relinking resolves it, and moving folders intact keeps that process simple.
