Chapter Eight, Section Eight

Delivering a Final Mix

Part VIII of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 051 / 095
Depends On 8.7
Read Time ~7 Min

Every chapter in this part has been building toward a mix that lives inside a Resolve project — audible on playback, correct in the Fairlight mixer, checked against a loudness meter. None of that is a deliverable yet. A mix that only exists inside the project that built it can't be handed to a broadcaster, uploaded to a platform, or opened by anyone without that exact project file. This closing chapter of Part VIII covers what it actually takes to turn a finished mix into files other people and other systems can use.

Stems: Delivering More Than a Single Mixed File

The simplest deliverable is a single stereo or surround file with everything already combined — dialogue, music, and effects baked into one track. Many delivery contexts need more than that: a broadcaster or a foreign-language distributor commonly requires stems, separate mixed-down files for each major category — a dialogue stem, a music stem, an effects stem — kept apart specifically so a later dub or remix can replace just one category without rebuilding the whole mix from scratch. Chapter 8.7's category-organized tracks and busses pay off directly here: because dialogue, music, and effects were already routed through their own busses, printing separate stems is a matter of soloing each bus in turn during export, not reconstructing an organizational scheme that was never there.

The Print Master: One Definitive Reference File

Alongside stems, most deliveries also call for a print master — the single, fully mixed reference file representing exactly how the finished project should sound, used as the source of truth against which every other deliverable gets checked. It's this file, not any individual stem, that gets measured against the loudness targets Chapter 8.4 established, because it's the version an actual audience will actually hear. Keeping a clearly labeled print master alongside your stems, rather than treating one of many mixdown attempts as an assumed final, avoids a surprisingly common and avoidable mistake: delivering the wrong version because nothing distinguished it from earlier passes.

Checking the Numbers One Last Time

Before anything ships, the print master gets a final pass against Chapter 8.4's loudness meter — confirming the integrated LUFS reading and true peak ceiling actually match whatever standard the delivery destination requires, not just approximately, but within the tolerance that standard specifies. This is a deliberately unglamorous final step, easy to treat as a formality after the real creative work of mixing is done, and skipping it is exactly how a mix that sounded right in the room ends up rejected by an automated ingest check or corrected by someone else's automatic loudness processor, which routinely produces a worse result than getting it right beforehand.

A mix isn't finished when it sounds right. It's finished when the print master, the stems, and the numbers on the meter all agree with each other and with whatever the destination actually requires.

Matching Delivery Specs to the Destination

Different destinations expect different technical packaging on top of the loudness targets already covered — sample rate, bit depth, channel configuration, and file format all vary by broadcaster, platform, or client, in the same way Chapter 2.7's project settings varied by the technical needs of a given production. A print master exported at the wrong sample rate or the wrong channel layout is just as much a failed delivery as one at the wrong loudness, even though it's a different kind of mistake. Confirming these specs against the destination's actual requirements, rather than assuming a sensible-sounding default will be accepted everywhere, belongs in the same final checklist as the loudness pass.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Stems — separate dialogue, music, and effects files — let a mix be adjusted or redubbed later without rebuilding it from scratch.
  • A clearly labeled print master, not an assumed-final mixdown attempt, should be the one definitive reference file delivered.
  • A final loudness check against the actual delivery standard is not a formality — skipping it is how mixes get rejected or auto-corrected.
  • Sample rate, bit depth, channel layout, and file format must match the destination's actual specs, not a generic sensible default.
