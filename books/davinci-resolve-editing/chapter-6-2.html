Chapter Six, Section Two

Syncing Angles: Timecode, Audio, and Waveform

Part VI of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 035 / 095
Depends On 6.1, 3.3
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 6.1 described a multicam clip as a commitment: several angles bundled into one object with a switchable angle property. That object doesn't exist yet — building it is the actual, practical first step of Part VI, and it depends entirely on getting the angles aligned correctly first. The two methods available are the same two Chapter 3.3 introduced for the Sync Bin, timecode and audio waveform, but here they're doing permanent work rather than a temporary review alignment, and it's worth understanding exactly how each one behaves before trusting it with hours of footage.

Creating a Multicam Clip from Selected Sources

A multicam clip is created by selecting the individual angle clips in the Media Pool — or, conveniently, directly from an existing Sync Bin grouping, since the alignment work Chapter 3.3 described transfers straight into the new clip — and choosing to create a multicam clip from that selection. Resolve then asks which sync method to use, and generates a single new clip in the Media Pool containing every selected angle, with each source clip's original name typically becoming that angle's label inside the new object. Nothing about this step is destructive to the original clips; they remain untouched and independently usable elsewhere in the project even after being folded into a multicam clip.

Timecode Sync: When It Just Works

Timecode sync aligns every angle using each clip's embedded timecode metadata, matching the exact same instant across sources the way a shared production clock would. When every camera and audio recorder on a shoot has been jam-synced or fed timecode from a common generator, this method is close to instantaneous and essentially perfectly accurate — there's no analysis to run, just metadata to read and match. It's the method worth actively planning for at the production stage precisely because of how reliably fast and accurate it makes this step later, in the edit.

Timecode sync isn't more sophisticated than waveform sync — it's simpler. It works by trusting metadata that was set correctly on set. When that trust is justified, nothing beats it for speed and precision.

Waveform Sync: The Reliable Fallback

Without shared timecode — the more common situation on smaller or faster-moving productions — Resolve can instead sync angles by analyzing and matching their audio waveforms, exactly as the Sync Bin does for review. Every angle needs a usable audio track for this to work, even a rough on-camera scratch track is generally sufficient, since the analysis is listening for a matching acoustic event across sources rather than judging audio quality. A single sharp, shared sound — a clap, a door closing, a burst of dialogue everyone's microphone picked up — is usually all the algorithm needs to lock every angle into place, and it typically finds that alignment point automatically without requiring you to identify it manually first.

Mixed Frame Rates and Sync Edge Cases

Two situations are worth knowing about before they surprise you mid-project. First, angles shot at different frame rates can still be synced and cut between, but Resolve has to conform one against the other to keep them aligned, which introduces a small amount of interpretation on whichever angle doesn't match your timeline's frame rate — worth being aware of if a mixed-frame-rate shoot needs to hold up under close scrutiny. Second, sync accuracy on long, unbroken takes can drift slightly on some camera and recorder combinations, even after a clean initial waveform match, because internal clocks on separate devices don't run at perfectly identical speeds over extended periods. Resolve's multicam clip lets you nudge an individual angle's offset manually after the fact, which is the direct, practical fix on the rare take where drift becomes audible or visible rather than something to solve by re-syncing from scratch.

With sync handled, the multicam clip is a complete, ready object — angles aligned, bundled, and sitting in the Media Pool as a single item. Chapter 6.3 picks up exactly there, covering the part of Part VI's promise that hasn't been shown yet: actually opening that clip in its dedicated viewer and cutting between angles in real time.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • A multicam clip is built by selecting angle clips — or an existing Sync Bin grouping — and choosing a sync method; the originals stay untouched.
  • Timecode sync is fast and exact when every source shares accurate timecode from production — plan for it on set when possible.
  • Waveform sync needs only a usable audio track per angle and one shared acoustic event to lock alignment reliably.
  • Mixed frame rates and long-take clock drift are known edge cases — manual per-angle offset adjustment is the direct fix.
