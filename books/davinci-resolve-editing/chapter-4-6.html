Chapter Four, Section Six

Linking, Grouping, and Compound Clips

Part IV of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 025 / 095
Depends On 4.2, 4.4
Read Time ~8 Min

So far, this part has treated clips mostly as individual objects — one clip, one track, one set of edits. Real sequences quickly need something more: a video clip that should move together with its own audio, several clips that need to shift together as a unit, or a whole cluster of clips that would be easier to manage as one thing rather than many. Resolve gives you three distinct tools for this, each solving a related but genuinely different version of the problem, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion worth heading off directly.

Linked Clips: Video and Audio Moving Together by Default

When you import a clip that has both video and audio recorded together — the normal case for most camera footage — Resolve treats its video and audio components as linked by default. Trim the video, and the linked audio trims identically. Move the clip, and both halves move together. This is the sensible default precisely because it's what you want the overwhelming majority of the time: a person's video and their synchronized voice shouldn't drift apart from an accidental trim to just one half.

Linking can be deliberately broken when needed — a split edit, where a clip's audio starts or ends slightly before or after its video, is a genuinely common editorial technique, particularly in dialogue-driven scenes, and Chapter 8.1 picks this up again from the audio side. Breaking a link temporarily for this kind of intentional offset is normal; the useful default is simply that linking exists so this kind of drift doesn't happen by accident.

Linking keeps video and audio together because they usually belong together. Grouping keeps unrelated clips together because you've decided, temporarily, that they should move as one. The two solve different problems and shouldn't be confused for each other.

Grouping: Multiple Clips, Moved as One, Without Merging

Grouping is a separate mechanism: selecting multiple clips — potentially on different tracks, potentially not adjacent to each other — and telling Resolve to treat them as a coordinated unit for certain operations, typically moving them together. Unlike linking, grouping isn't specific to a video-and-audio pair; it's a general tool for saying "these clips are related right now, and I want to act on them together" without permanently merging them into anything new. Ungroup them later, and each clip returns to being fully independent, exactly as it was before.

This is useful in situations like temporarily shifting a whole cluster of B-roll and its accompanying music cue together earlier in a sequence, or repositioning several graphic elements that need to stay aligned relative to each other as a set moves. The relationship a group represents is deliberately loose and reversible — a working convenience, not a structural commitment.

Compound Clips: Merging Multiple Clips Into One New Object

Compound clips go further than grouping, and it's worth being precise about the difference. Selecting several clips and creating a compound clip from them bundles them into a genuinely new, single clip — one that appears in your Media Pool as its own item, with its own thumbnail, and behaves on the timeline like any other single clip: one block, one set of trim handles, one thing to select and move. Internally, that compound clip still contains its original multiple clips and their relative timing, editable by opening the compound clip itself, but from the outside, on the timeline where it's placed, it presents as one unified object.

This is the right tool once a cluster of clips has stopped being "several related things I'm still arranging" and become "one finished unit I want to treat as a single building block" — a fully assembled lower-third graphic made of several layered elements, for instance, that you now want to drop into multiple places across a sequence, or move around as easily as a single clip, without the burden of every internal element being independently selectable every time you touch it.

Choosing Between the Three

A short, practical rule ties these together: linking is usually automatic and rarely something you set up deliberately, since it comes from how footage was recorded. Grouping is for a temporary, working relationship between clips you're still actively arranging — reversible, lightweight, and not a new object. Compound clips are for a settled, finished relationship you want to treat as one thing going forward — a genuinely new clip, reusable, and a bit heavier to set up and reason about. If you find yourself repeatedly regrouping the same clips every session, that's usually a signal you actually want a compound clip instead.

Chapter 4.7 closes out Part IV with a structurally related but distinct idea: nested timelines, which take the compound clip concept one step further by letting an entire separate timeline — not just a bundle of clips — sit inside another timeline as if it were a single clip.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Linked video and audio move and trim together by default, preventing accidental sync drift — break the link only when you deliberately want an offset.
  • Grouping is a temporary, reversible relationship between clips you're still arranging — nothing merges, and ungrouping restores full independence.
  • A compound clip merges several clips into one genuinely new, reusable Media Pool object — the right choice once a cluster is finished, not still in flux.
  • Repeatedly regrouping the same clips is a signal you probably want a compound clip instead.
