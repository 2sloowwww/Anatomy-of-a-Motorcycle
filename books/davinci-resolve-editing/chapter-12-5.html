Chapter Twelve, Section Five

Working Across Editorial, Color, and Sound Teams

Part XII of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 076 / 095
Depends On 12.4
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 12.1 opened this part with an image worth returning to now: an editor on the Edit page, a colorist on the Color page, a sound mixer in Fairlight, all occupying the same project at once. The chapters since have supplied the mechanics that make that image real rather than aspirational — a shared database, bin-level locking, workflows that hold up across distance. This closing chapter of Part XII asks the practical question underneath all of it: what does it actually take for editorial, color, and sound to work well together inside that same shared project, not just technically, but as three teams with genuinely different rhythms?

Three Teams, One Project, Different Rhythms

Editorial, color, and sound don't move at the same pace, and pretending otherwise is where most coordination problems start. Editorial is inherently iterative and fast-changing — scenes get recut, reordered, and trimmed repeatedly, often daily, right up until very late in a schedule. Color moves more deliberately, in considered passes, and generally wants picture that has settled at least at the scene level before investing real grading time in it. Sound layers in throughout, from rough temp cues early on to a final mix late, but a full mix specifically depends on picture timing that has genuinely stopped moving. A shared database doesn't erase these different rhythms — it just means all three are now visibly happening inside one project at once, rather than hidden behind separate file handoffs.

Picture Lock Isn't All-or-Nothing Anymore

The traditional idea of a single, hard picture lock — one moment where editorial stops entirely and color and sound begin — assumes exactly the sequential handoff model Chapter 12.1 argued against. Concurrent workflows replace that all-or-nothing moment with something more granular: individual scenes or reels can lock, in the Chapter 12.3 sense, independently of one another. A colorist can begin grading Scene 4 the moment its timeline settles and gets locked, while editorial keeps actively recutting Scene 9 in a different bin entirely. Picture lock, under this model, becomes a rolling, scene-by-scene status rather than a single date circled on a schedule — genuinely useful, provided everyone understands that "locked" now describes a specific piece of the project, not the whole of it.

Conform and Version Drift Across Specialties

Working from one shared, live project — rather than exporting XML or EDL files between separate editorial, color, and sound systems — removes an entire category of classic conform problems. There's no separate re-import step where a colorist's grade might silently detach from an editor's latest cut, because both are looking at the same underlying timeline rather than disconnected copies of it. What this doesn't remove is the human problem underneath: if editorial does recut a scene a colorist had already treated as locked, that change still needs to be communicated clearly, not just technically propagated. This is exactly where Chapter 12.4's markers-as-asynchronous-review habit earns its keep again — a note left directly on the affected scene, visible to whoever opens it next, is a far more reliable way to flag a recut than hoping someone happens to notice.

The technology makes concurrent work possible. It doesn't make it automatic — a team still needs to agree, explicitly, on who owns a scene at any given moment, and what "locked" actually means when nothing is truly final until delivery.

Roles, Not Just Access

It's worth closing Part XII on a point that's easy to lose amid all this mechanism: a shared database, working locks, and solid remote tools solve the technical half of team-based post-production, but they don't by themselves solve the organizational half. A team still needs clear, explicit conventions — who owns a given scene at a given moment, what "locked" is understood to mean day to day, how a recut on treated picture actually gets flagged. Chapter 2.2's bin structure and Chapter 12.3's locking give a team the tools to build those conventions cleanly; they don't substitute for the team actually agreeing on them.

That closes Part XII. Between why Resolve was built around concurrent access at all, the shared database and Collaboration Mode underneath it, bin-level locking, workflows that reach across distance, and now this chapter's look at three specialist teams sharing one rolling production, the full shape of studio collaboration in Resolve is on the table. All of that shared activity — several people, several pages, all hitting the same project and the same storage at once — is exactly what raises the stakes on a question Part XIII turns to next: what actually determines whether a Resolve system holds up smoothly under that load, or starts to strain under it.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Editorial, color, and sound move at genuinely different paces — a shared project makes that visible rather than removing it.
  • Picture lock becomes a rolling, scene-by-scene status under concurrent workflows, not one single date the whole project waits on.
  • A shared live project removes classic file-based conform drift, but a recut on "locked" picture still needs to be actively communicated.
  • Technical mechanisms enable concurrent teamwork; explicit team conventions about ownership are still what keeps it from becoming chaos.
