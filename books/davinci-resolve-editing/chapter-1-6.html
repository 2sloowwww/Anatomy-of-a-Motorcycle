Setting Up Resolve for the First Time

Part I of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 006 / 095
Depends On 1.4, 1.5
Read Time ~8 Min

Everything so far in Part I has been conceptual — how nonlinear editing works, why Resolve is shaped the way it is, what nodes and color management actually mean. This chapter is the first purely practical one in the book: getting Resolve installed, opening it for the first time, and creating a first project with settings that won't cause problems three weeks into a job. None of this is difficult, but a few early choices are much cheaper to get right at the start than to fix after you've built hours of work on top of them.

Free Versus Studio: Knowing Which You Need

Resolve ships in two versions: a free version and a paid Studio version. The free version is genuinely capable — it's a full nonlinear editor, colorist tool, and audio workstation, not a stripped demo — but it caps output resolution below 4K in some releases, limits certain noise reduction and advanced Fairlight features, and doesn't support some collaboration and multi-GPU workflows covered later in Part XII and XIII. For learning the craft covered in Parts I through IX, the free version is entirely sufficient. If your delivery requirements or collaboration needs eventually bump into one of its limits, upgrading later doesn't cost you any of the work you've already done — projects carry forward cleanly.

The Project Manager: Your Library of Projects

The first screen you'll see after launching Resolve is the Project Manager, not an empty timeline. This is deliberate, and it reflects something worth understanding early: a "project" in Resolve isn't a single file sitting in a folder the way a document is in most software. Projects live inside a database — by default, a local one Resolve manages for you — and the Project Manager is your interface to that database: creating new projects, opening existing ones, and organizing them into folders. Chapter 2.1 covers what a project actually contains in more depth; for now, know that the Project Manager is where every session with Resolve begins.

Creating a new project takes seconds. The two or three settings you choose in that moment — resolution, frame rate, color science — are the ones most expensive to change later, once real work is built on top of them.

Creating a First Project: The Settings That Matter Immediately

When you create a new project, Resolve asks for very little up front — mainly a name. The settings that actually matter live in the Project Settings panel, and while Chapter 2.7 covers this checklist in full, three of them are worth setting correctly from your very first project, because getting them wrong is the single most common source of frustration for beginners.

Timeline resolution and frame rate. These should match your footage, not an arbitrary default. If you shot at 3840×2160, 23.976fps, your project's timeline settings should match that. A mismatch doesn't necessarily break anything outright, but it introduces resizing and frame-rate conversion that can cost quality and cause playback stutter you'll spend time troubleshooting later, without an obvious cause.

Color science and color management mode. Chapter 1.4 explained why this matters conceptually — this is where that concept becomes a real setting. If you're not ready to make an informed choice yet, Resolve Color Management with its default settings is a reasonable, safe starting point that avoids the flat, unmanaged log image problem from Chapter 1.4. You'll revisit this properly in Part IX.

Working and storage locations. Where Resolve stores cache files, and where your Media Pool expects to find your footage, matters more than it seems like it should. Chapter 2.8 covers backup and archiving in depth, but even for a first project, it's worth knowing where things are being written, rather than accepting defaults blindly and discovering later that cache files have quietly filled a drive you needed space on.

A Few Preferences Worth Checking Early

Beyond project-level settings, Resolve also has application-wide preferences that apply across every project. You don't need to tour all of them now, but two are worth a glance in your first session: the keyboard layout, particularly if you're coming from another editor and want a familiar shortcut scheme (Resolve ships with alternate layouts for exactly this reason), and the playback and GPU settings, which Chapter 13.2 revisits in depth once performance becomes a more pressing concern.

What "First Time Setup" Actually Means Here

The goal of this chapter wasn't to walk you through every checkbox in Resolve's preferences — that would be tedious and largely unnecessary, since most defaults are sensible. The goal was to make sure the handful of settings that are expensive to change later — resolution, frame rate, color management, storage locations — get your attention before you start building real work on top of them. Everything else can be safely learned as you go, adjusted later, or left at its default indefinitely.

With Part I's conceptual foundation in place and a correctly configured first project open, you're genuinely ready to start editing. Part II picks up immediately from here, with a full look at organizing footage inside the Media Pool — the first real, hands-on skill in this book.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • The free version of Resolve is a complete, capable editor for learning the craft — Studio's extra features mostly matter for high-end delivery and collaboration, covered later.
  • A Resolve "project" lives in a managed database, not a single file — the Project Manager is your library and starting point every session.
  • Timeline resolution and frame rate should match your footage, and color management should be configured deliberately, from the very first project you create.
  • Most preferences are safe to leave at default and learn as you go — only a small handful of project settings are genuinely expensive to fix after the fact.
