Chapter Ten, Section Five

Building a Simple Title or Graphic in Fusion

Part X of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 064 / 095
Depends On 10.4
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 7.3 introduced Fusion titles from the Edit page's side — the third, most capable option when Text+ and templates both fall short, described there mostly in terms of what it made possible rather than how it actually gets built. This chapter finally builds one, node by node, using the vocabulary Chapters 10.2 through 10.4 have spent the rest of this part establishing: the Merge node, keying and mattes, and tracked motion, now applied to a genuinely constructive task rather than a corrective one.

Starting From Nothing: The Background and Text+ Nodes

A Fusion title composition, at its simplest, starts with two source nodes and nothing else: a Background node, generating a flat color or gradient with no footage behind it at all, and a Text+ node, generating typed text with its own font, size, and color controls. This is worth pausing on, because it's the same Text+ tool Chapter 7.3 already introduced on the Edit page — inside Fusion, it isn't a different tool, just the same generator exposed as one node among many rather than packaged behind a single Inspector panel.

Combining Them: The Merge Node Returns

With a background and some text as two independent sources, Chapter 10.2's Merge node does exactly the job it was introduced to do: combining them into one output, with the text's position, scale, and blend behavior all adjustable at the Merge itself rather than baked into either source node. This is a genuinely small composition — three nodes, one straight chain — and it's worth noticing how directly it maps onto Chapter 10.2's claim that most real compositions are close to a straight line. A title this simple doesn't need branching structure; it needs exactly the serial thinking that chapter said was the everyday case.

A three-node title — background, text, merge — is Fusion at its most approachable. Everything this part has covered so far shows up here in miniature: a source, another source, and one explicit step that puts them together.

Adding a Second Layer: A Graphic Element

Most real titles need more than text alone — a logo, an underline bar, a simple shape behind the type for legibility. Each additional element is another source node, and each one joins the composite through its own Merge, chained onto the output of the previous one. A logo might merge onto the background first, with the text merged on top of that combined result second, giving deliberate control over which element sits in front of which — precisely the ordering discipline Chapter 10.2 described as still mattering inside a graph, even without a fixed layer list enforcing it automatically.

Giving the Title Motion

A static title is often enough, but animated motion — text sliding or fading in, a bar wiping across before the type appears — draws on the same keyframing concept Chapter 7.5 introduced on the Edit page, expressed inside Fusion through its own Spline editor rather than the Inspector's simple stopwatch icon. The underlying logic is identical: a value at one point in time, a different value at another, with everything in between calculated automatically. What Fusion's Spline editor adds beyond the Edit page's basic keyframing is finer control over the actual shape of that interpolation curve — useful once a title's motion needs to feel more specific than a generic ease in or out.

Attaching a Title to Moving Footage

When a title needs to sit convincingly inside a moving shot rather than floating flatly over it — a label that appears to be stuck to a moving object, a caption anchored to a specific point in a handheld frame — Chapter 10.4's tracking data drives that directly. The tracker measures the target point's motion; the title's Merge node reads that motion data as its position input instead of a fixed, static value. This is the payoff moment for treating tracking as data rather than as a standalone effect back in Chapter 10.4: once motion exists as data, anything in the composition, including a title built this chapter's way, can be driven by it.

That completes the hands-on half of Part X — isolating a subject, combining elements, tracking motion, and now building a real graphic from those pieces. Chapter 10.6 closes the part by returning to a judgment call this chapter has been implicitly making the whole time: when a title, effect, or composite genuinely needs this much machinery, and when it doesn't.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • A basic Fusion title is a Background node and a Text+ node joined by a Merge — the same three-part pattern Chapter 10.2 described as typical.
  • Each additional graphic element joins through its own Merge, chained onto the previous result — order is still a deliberate choice.
  • Fusion's Spline editor animates a title using the same keyframe logic as Chapter 7.5, with finer control over the interpolation curve itself.
  • A tracker's motion data can drive a title's position directly, letting a graphic ride real footage rather than sit flatly on top of it.
