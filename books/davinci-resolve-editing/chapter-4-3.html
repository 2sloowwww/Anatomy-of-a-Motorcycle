The Toolbar: Selection, Trim, Blade, and More

Part IV of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 022 / 095
Depends On 4.1
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 3.4 flagged, without fully explaining, that the Edit page requires selecting a specific tool before certain actions become available — a deliberate trade against the Cut page's grab-anything-directly speed. This chapter is that explanation: a tour of the Edit page's toolbar, and what changes about how the timeline responds to your mouse depending on which tool is currently active.

Why Tool Modes Exist at All

The timeline is a dense, layered surface — multiple tracks, edit points close together, clips of varying lengths sitting right next to gaps. A single click near an edit point could plausibly mean several different things: select the clip, trim the edit point, or cut a new edit point at that exact spot. Rather than guessing your intent from context, the Edit page asks you to declare it up front by choosing a tool, and then interprets every subsequent click and drag according to that tool's specific behavior. It's a small amount of upfront overhead in exchange for a large amount of precision and predictability once a sequence gets complex.

The Selection Tool: The Default, General-Purpose Mode

The Selection tool, typically the default, is the general-purpose mode for clicking and dragging clips: selecting them, moving them to a new position, or making a basic trim by dragging an edit point's edge. It's the tool you'll return to most often between more specialized operations, and it behaves closest to what a newcomer might instinctively expect clicking on a timeline to do.

The Trim Tool: Precision Adjustments to Edit Points

Where the Selection tool's trimming is quick and general, the dedicated Trim tool gives you more deliberate control over exactly how an edit point adjustment behaves — the starting point for the full vocabulary of ripple, roll, slip, and slide trims that Part V covers as its own complete subject. You don't need that full vocabulary yet to understand the toolbar's role here: selecting the Trim tool tells the timeline you're specifically here to adjust boundaries between clips, precisely, rather than to move or select clips more generally.

Switching tools isn't switching software modes for their own sake. Each tool tells the timeline what a click and a drag should mean right now — select, move, trim, or cut — and picking the right one before you act is what makes the interaction predictable.

The Blade Tool: Cutting a Clip in Two

The Blade tool does one specific thing: clicking on a clip with it active splits that clip into two separate clips at exactly the point you clicked, each now independently selectable, movable, and trimmable. This is a genuinely common operation — isolating a specific moment inside a longer clip so it can be treated separately, removing an unwanted section from the middle of a clip, or preparing a clip for a cutaway insert. It's a simple tool, but an essential one, and it comes up constantly enough in real editing that it's worth knowing its dedicated toolbar icon rather than hunting for a menu command each time.

Other Toolbar Modes Worth Knowing By Sight

A few additional tools round out the toolbar, each solving a narrower, specific problem: a Dynamic Trim mode, which Part V revisits for real-time, playback-driven trimming rather than static drag adjustments; a Zoom tool, for direct control over the timeline's zoom level beyond the slider Chapter 4.1 mentioned; and a Hand or Scroll tool, for panning across a long timeline without changing zoom level or selection at all. None of these need mastering on first exposure — knowing they exist, and roughly what problem each one solves, is enough for now, with the specifics filled in as later chapters actually put them to use.

Building the Habit of Checking Your Tool

The most common early mistake with the Edit page's toolbar isn't misusing a specific tool — it's forgetting which tool is currently active and being surprised when a click does something other than what you expected. Chapter 1.5's advice about confirming which page and which viewer you're looking at applies here too, extended one level further: on the Edit page specifically, it's worth building the reflex of glancing at the toolbar before an unfamiliar or high-stakes action, confirming the active tool matches your actual intent, rather than assuming.

With the toolbar's modes established, Chapter 4.4 puts them to immediate use, covering the four fundamental ways a clip actually gets placed onto a timeline in the first place — insert, overwrite, append, and replace — each with a distinctly different effect on everything already there.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Tool modes exist so a click near an edit point has one predictable meaning, rather than requiring the software to guess your intent.
  • Selection is the general-purpose default; the dedicated Trim tool is the entry point into Part V's fuller trim vocabulary.
  • The Blade tool splits one clip into two at the clicked point — simple, but essential and frequently used.
  • Build the habit of confirming your active tool before an unfamiliar action — the most common toolbar mistake is forgetting which mode is on.
