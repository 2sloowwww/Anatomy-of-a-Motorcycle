Chapter Seven, Section Two

Cross Dissolves, Cuts, and When Not to Use Either

Part VII of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 040 / 095
Depends On 7.1
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 7.1 established what a cross dissolve structurally requires and what it communicates. This chapter turns that into an actual editorial decision — not just whether a dissolve is technically possible at a given cut, but whether it's the right choice, how long it should run when it is, and two situations where the real answer is neither a dissolve nor a hard cut.

Dissolve Length Is an Editorial Choice, Not a Default

Resolve applies a default duration to every dissolve you add, and it's tempting to treat that number as correct simply because it's what appears automatically. It's a starting point, not a rule. A dissolve's length is itself communicating something: a longer dissolve reads as a slower, more deliberate passage — real time elapsing, a meaningful shift — while a very short dissolve barely registers as an effect at all, functioning more like a slight softening of a cut than an overt signal of change. Matching that length to what the moment actually needs, rather than accepting whatever duration the effect defaulted to, is the difference between a dissolve that supports a moment and one that just sits there looking like an unexamined habit.

When a Hard Cut Is Correct — Which Is Most of the Time

Chapter 7.1 made the case that a hard cut and a dissolve carry different meaning, not just a different look. The practical consequence: most cuts in most sequences should remain hard cuts, because most cuts are not actually depicting a passage of time or a shift in reality — they're simply moving from one continuous moment to the next. Dialogue exchanges, continuous action, anything happening in one unbroken stretch of time all call for the immediacy a hard cut preserves. Reaching for a dissolve there doesn't add polish; it quietly, incorrectly tells the audience that something shifted when nothing did.

A dissolve on every cut isn't a style — it's the same message repeated at every single edit point, whether or not that message is actually true at each one. Most cuts want the immediacy only a hard cut gives them.

The Middle Ground: Staggering Cuts Instead of Blending Them

Some moments feel like they need softening without actually calling for a literal image blend, and the better tool there is often an L-cut or J-cut — letting a clip's audio and video cut at different points rather than at the same frame, so the next scene's sound arrives slightly before its picture, or the previous scene's audio lingers slightly after its picture has already changed. This produces a genuinely smoother-feeling transition than a hard cut without ever blending two images together, and it avoids implying the kind of time-passage a dissolve would. It's worth having as a default instinct before reaching for a dissolve: ask whether staggering sound and picture solves the roughness first.

Fades to Black: A Related but Distinct Signal

A fade to or from black is structurally a dissolve, but against solid black rather than another clip, and it's worth keeping conceptually separate from a cross dissolve between two content clips because it signals something stronger. Where a cross dissolve softly connects two moments to each other, a fade to black is a full stop — the strongest gap this book has covered, appropriate for a chapter break, a scene ending, or the close of a whole piece, not for a routine transition mid-scene. Using it at the wrong scale, mid-sequence rather than at a genuine structural boundary, overstates the break it's actually marking.

With transitions covered from structure through practical choice, Chapter 7.3 turns to a different layer entirely: titles, and the range of tools Resolve offers for building them, from simple Text+ generators through full templates and Fusion-built titles.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • A dissolve's length communicates pacing — match it to the moment rather than accepting the default duration unexamined.
  • Most cuts should stay hard cuts — dissolving a cut that isn't depicting time passage sends an inaccurate signal.
  • An L-cut or J-cut often smooths a rough moment better than a dissolve, without blending images or implying time passing.
  • A fade to black is a full stop, not a routine transition — reserve it for genuine structural boundaries, not mid-scene softening.
