Chapter Nine, Section One

What Color Grading Actually Does for an Edit

Part IX of XVI
Category Foundational
Sheet 052 / 095
Depends On 8.8
Read Time ~6 Min

Chapter 8.8 closed out the audio side of this book with a final mix ready to sit under picture. The picture itself hasn't had that same closing pass yet. Whatever color adjustments happened during the edit, if any, were provisional — quick fixes made under deadline pressure, with one eye always on whether a shot might still get trimmed, replaced, or cut entirely. Now that both picture and sound are locked, the project gets its actual color pass. Part IX is about what that pass is, what it's for, and why it belongs this late rather than earlier.

The Picture's Last Major Pass

Grading arrives last for the same reason final audio work does: it's expensive to redo, and editorial decisions upstream of it change constantly right up until they don't. A shot that gets meticulously graded on Monday and cut from the sequence on Wednesday was time spent on nothing. Waiting until picture lock — the point at which the arrangement of shots is genuinely settled — means every hour spent grading goes toward a shot that will actually ship. This isn't a scheduling convention imposed from outside the craft; it's the same logic that has run through every part of this book, applied to color specifically: build structure first, refine what survives.

Two Jobs Hiding Under One Name

"Color grading" gets used loosely to describe two genuinely different jobs, and separating them now will save confusion through the rest of this part. The first is correction: making shots that were captured under different conditions — different cameras, different exposures, different lighting setups across a shoot day — agree with each other well enough that a viewer never notices they came from separate sources. Correction is a technical problem with a mostly objective target. The second job is look: pushing a scene's color and contrast toward a specific creative intention, a mood, a genre convention, an emotional register. Look is subjective, and reasonable graders can disagree about it.

Most real grading sessions do both, often on the same shot, often close together. But they're not the same operation, and conflating them is a common source of frustration for new graders — chasing a "look" on a shot that hasn't been corrected yet means you're building a creative choice on top of an inconsistency you haven't actually solved, and the result rarely holds up once you compare it to the next shot in the sequence.

Correction makes shots agree with each other. Look makes shots agree with an intention. They happen in the same sessions, often on the same shot, but they are not the same job — and most grading frustration traces back to running one while thinking you're running the other.

Why Grading Waits This Long

There's a second, more practical reason grading sits at Part IX rather than earlier in this book, beyond simply following picture lock. Chapter 1.3 introduced the node graph as an idea that runs through Resolve's Color and Fusion pages, and promised this part would make it concrete once you had a reason to actually build one. Chapter 1.4 explained color management — why footage looks flat and wrong before a project's color space translation is configured — and deferred the hands-on side of that translation to this same part, for the same reason: those concepts land better once you've cut real footage and have real material to point them at. Everything Part IX teaches was, in a real sense, already set up for you before you finished reading Chapter One.

The Shape of Part IX

The chapters ahead build in a deliberate order, each one depending on the last. Chapter 9.2 covers the scopes — waveform, vectorscope, histogram — that replace guessing with measurement. Chapter 9.3 returns to the node graph from Chapter 1.3 and makes it concrete on the Color page specifically. Chapters 9.4 and 9.5 cover the two levels of correction: primary, which touches the whole frame, and secondary, which touches a chosen part of it. Chapter 9.6 applies both to the specific, unglamorous, constant task of making shots from different sources match. Chapter 9.7 covers LUTs, color space, and getting the right image out the door. Chapter 9.8 closes the part by walking that entire sequence start to finish, on one project, in order.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Grading is the picture's final major pass, arriving after picture lock for the same reason final audio work does — it's expensive to redo on shots that might not survive the cut.
  • "Color grading" hides two distinct jobs — correction, making shots agree with each other, and look, making shots agree with an intention.
  • Chasing a look before correction is finished builds a creative choice on top of an inconsistency that hasn't actually been solved.
  • Part IX builds in order: scopes, the node graph, primary correction, secondary correction, matching, LUTs and delivery, and a complete workflow tying it together.
