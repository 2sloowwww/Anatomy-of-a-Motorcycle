Chapter Three, Section Five

When to Use Cut vs. Edit

Part III of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 019 / 095
Depends On 3.1, 3.4
Read Time ~6 Min

This closing chapter of Part III doesn't introduce a new tool. It answers the practical question the previous four chapters have been building toward: given that Cut and Edit are both genuinely capable, full front doors into the same timeline, how do you actually decide which one to open on any given day, or at any given moment within a single project?

The Wrong Way to Frame the Question

It's tempting to think of this as a skill-level question — Cut for beginners, Edit for professionals — but that framing doesn't hold up, and Chapter 3.1 already explained why: the Cut page isn't a simplified Edit page, it's a different tool optimized for a different kind of pressure. Plenty of highly experienced editors reach for Cut constantly, specifically because their work involves exactly the volume and speed pressures it's built for. The right question isn't "which page am I skilled enough for." It's "which problem am I actually solving right now."

Reach for Cut When Speed and Volume Dominate

The clearest signal that Cut is the right tool: you have a lot of footage, limited time, and the immediate goal is getting a coherent structure down rather than a polished result. Same-day turnaround work. High-volume unscripted or documentary footage where you're building a rough assembly from dozens of hours of material. Quick social content where the entire piece will live or die on pacing decisions made fast, with little need for complex layering. In all of these, Source Tape's continuous browsing, the Sync Bin's fast comparisons, and the trim timeline's frictionless adjustments — Chapters 3.2 through 3.4 — directly address the actual bottleneck: time.

The real decision isn't Cut versus Edit as competing tools. It's rough structure versus precise refinement as two different phases of the same project — and each phase has a page built specifically for it.

Reach for Edit When Precision and Depth Dominate

The signal flips once a sequence needs things the Cut page deliberately doesn't offer in depth: multiple video and audio tracks working together, precise, deliberate control over specific trim types, custom transitions and titles beyond the Cut page's built-in defaults, or careful frame-accurate timing work. A scripted narrative scene with layered dialogue, music, and sound effects. A sequence requiring exact, considered pacing down to individual frames. Complex multi-track compositing leading into Fusion work covered in Part X. These all call for the full toolset Part IV through Part VII are built around.

The Common, Sensible Pattern: Both, in Sequence

For a large share of real projects, the honest answer isn't "Cut" or "Edit" — it's "Cut, then Edit." Build a rough assembly quickly on Cut, using Source Tape and fast placement to get the overall shape and pacing of a sequence down without getting bogged into precision too early. Then move to Edit once that structure exists, refining trims, adding the layered audio and visual work the rough pass didn't need yet, and applying the frame-accurate control a finished piece actually requires.

This pattern works cleanly specifically because of Chapter 1.2 and 3.1's core point, worth restating one final time here: nothing is lost or converted moving between the two pages, because there was never two separate things to reconcile. A rough cut built entirely on Cut opens on Edit exactly as it stands, ready for refinement, with every placement and trim intact.

Part III, Complete

That closes Part III. Between understanding why the Cut page exists at all, its tools for finding footage fast, its lightweight sync review, its frictionless trim timeline, and now a practical framework for choosing when to use it, Cut should feel like a genuine option in your toolkit rather than a page you skip past on the way to something more familiar. Part IV picks up next with the Edit page in full — the deeper toolset this chapter has referenced throughout, covered from the ground up starting with the anatomy of its timeline.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Choosing between Cut and Edit is about which problem you're solving right now, not about skill level — both are tools professionals rely on.
  • Cut wins when speed and volume dominate: same-day turnarounds, high-volume unscripted footage, fast-paced social content.
  • Edit wins when precision and depth dominate: multi-track work, deliberate trim control, custom titles and transitions, frame-accurate timing.
  • A common, effective pattern is both in sequence — rough assembly on Cut, refinement on Edit — with nothing lost moving between them.
