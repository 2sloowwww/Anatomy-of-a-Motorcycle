Chapter Eleven, Section Five

Delivering for Broadcast, Streaming, and Social

Part XI of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 070 / 095
Depends On 11.4
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 11.1 opened this part with the claim that one finished timeline routinely becomes several distinct deliverables. This chapter is where that claim gets specific. Broadcast, streaming, and social destinations don't just differ in codec preference — they answer to genuinely different standards, audiences, and technical gatekeepers, and treating them as interchangeable variations on the same export is where deliveries most often go wrong.

Broadcast: Strict Specs, Formal Quality Control

Broadcast delivery is the most rigid of the three, and deliberately so — a network or broadcaster typically mandates an exact codec, container, resolution, and frame rate, along with formal loudness and technical standards a delivery must pass before it airs at all. This isn't a suggestion to weigh against convenience; a broadcast QC process exists specifically to catch deviations from spec before they become an on-air problem, and a delivery that fails it is sent back, not aired with an asterisk. Audio compliance in particular is exacting here — loudness levels held to a specific, mandated standard, not just kept generally reasonable.

Streaming: Defined, but More Forgiving

Streaming platforms sit between broadcast's rigidity and social media's looseness. Major platforms publish real, specific technical delivery specs of their own — sometimes down to exact codec profiles and container structures — but the review process around them tends to be less formally adversarial than broadcast QC, and the range of acceptable input formats is often somewhat wider. The practical discipline is the same either way: check the actual, current spec for the specific platform rather than assuming a broadcast-grade or general-purpose render will simply be accepted as-is.

Three destinations, three different relationships to the word "correct." Broadcast enforces it formally before anything airs. Streaming defines it and expects you to meet it. Social barely defines it at all — and quietly punishes a file that doesn't fit anyway.

Social: Loosest Formal Rules, Most Practical Constraints

Social platforms are, on paper, the least formally strict of the three — there's rarely a QC process rejecting a file outright. In practice, they carry the most real-world constraints on how a video actually needs to be built to perform well: vertical or square aspect ratios rather than traditional widescreen framing, captions frequently burned in or otherwise embedded since so much social viewing happens muted, shorter overall durations, and aggressive platform-side compression that rewards a delivery already reasonably efficient going in. None of this is enforced by a rejection message the way broadcast QC is — it's enforced by how well or poorly the finished video actually performs once it's live, which is arguably a higher bar to guess right on the first attempt.

One Master, Many Outputs — In Practice

This is where Chapter 11.1's opening idea and Chapter 11.3's presets meet in a real workflow. A single graded, composited timeline commonly becomes a full-resolution broadcast or archival master, a platform-compliant streaming render, and one or more reframed, captioned social cutdowns — each one a distinct render job from the render queue, each ideally driven by its own saved preset rather than manually reconfigured from scratch. The timeline underneath all of them is identical; what differs, deliberately and by design, is everything Chapter 11.2 and this chapter have covered, tuned to each specific destination.

Color Space Deserves a Specific Mention Here

Chapter 1.4 introduced color management as a concept worth understanding before touching any specific tool, and destination delivery is exactly where that concept becomes unavoidable in practice. Broadcast specs commonly mandate a specific color space and gamma curve; streaming platforms increasingly support wider gamuts and high dynamic range as legitimate, separate deliverables; social platforms are generally far less particular, but still assume a standard, compatible color space by default. A render that's technically flawless in every other respect but delivered in the wrong color space can still look visibly wrong the moment it reaches its destination — a reminder that Part IX's grading work and Part XI's delivery work are connected all the way through to the final file.

With destination-specific delivery covered, the last piece of Part XI isn't about producing a file at all — it's about handing the project itself off to someone else. Chapter 11.6 closes both this part and this book's tour of Resolve's pages with exactly that.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Broadcast enforces exact specs and loudness standards through a formal QC process before anything airs.
  • Streaming platforms define real specs of their own, with a somewhat less rigid review process than broadcast.
  • Social platforms enforce almost nothing formally, but punish a poorly framed, uncaptioned, or inefficient delivery through performance instead.
  • One graded, composited timeline routinely becomes several destination-specific renders, each driven by its own preset.
