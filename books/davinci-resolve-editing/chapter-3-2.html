Chapter Three, Section Two

Source Tape and Finding Footage Fast

Part III of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 016 / 095
Depends On 3.1
Read Time ~7 Min

Before you can place a shot quickly, you have to find it quickly, and finding it is often the slower half of the job on a footage-heavy project. Opening clip after clip individually, one at a time, to check what's actually in each one, adds up fast across a bin with hundreds of takes. Source Tape is the Cut page's answer to exactly this problem, and it's worth understanding as a genuinely different way of browsing footage, not just a viewer with a longer scrubber bar.

One Continuous Strip, Instead of Many Separate Clips

Normally, browsing a bin means opening one clip at a time into a source viewer, watching or scrubbing through it, closing it, and opening the next. Source Tape changes the unit you're browsing: instead of one clip, it concatenates every clip in a selected bin — in bin order — into a single, continuous virtual tape you can scrub across as if it were one long recording. Reaching the end of one clip on that tape simply continues into the next, with no need to close anything and open something else.

This mirrors, deliberately, how editors used to work with actual videotape decks — shuttling across a long reel to find a moment, rather than jumping between discrete files. The metaphor is intentional: scrubbing continuously across footage is often simply a faster way for a human eye to scan for a specific moment than repeatedly opening and closing individual clips, especially when you're not entirely sure which specific clip contains what you're looking for.

Source Tape doesn't make any single clip easier to review. It makes an entire bin behave like one long recording — trading the overhead of opening clips one by one for a single, continuous scrub across everything at once.

Setting an In and Out Point Across Clip Boundaries

Because Source Tape treats the bin as one continuous strip, marking an in point in one clip and an out point somewhere later — potentially inside a different clip further down the tape — behaves naturally, the same way it would on a single long clip. This is particularly useful when a sequence needs a specific short moment and you're not certain, going in, exactly which clip it lives in; you can simply scrub until you find it, mark it, and move on, without first needing to identify and open the correct individual clip.

Built on Top of Chapter 2's Organization

Source Tape's usefulness is directly proportional to how well the underlying bin is organized — which is exactly why Part II's groundwork matters here, not just in the abstract. A Source Tape built from a well-organized bin, following Chapter 2.3's organizing principles, gives you a continuous scrub across footage that's already grouped meaningfully — by day, by camera, by scene. A Source Tape built from an unsorted dump of hundreds of unrelated clips is still technically one continuous strip, but scrubbing across it tells you far less, because there's no meaningful order underlying what you're scanning through.

This is also where smart bins from Chapter 2.4 become genuinely powerful in combination with Source Tape: point Source Tape at a smart bin built from a "flagged as select" search, and you get a continuous scrub across only the footage your team already identified as worth using — a dramatically faster way to review selects than scrubbing across everything shot that day, unfiltered.

Where This Leads Next

Source Tape solves finding footage within a single bin quickly. It doesn't, on its own, solve a related but distinct problem: what happens when the footage you need to review consists of multiple camera angles or audio sources of the exact same moment, and you need to see how they relate to each other in time. That's the job of the Sync Bin, which Chapter 3.3 covers next — a tool built specifically for reviewing multiple, time-aligned sources side by side, without the heavier commitment of formally grouping them into a multicam clip.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Source Tape concatenates a bin's clips into one continuous, scrubbable strip — no opening and closing individual clips one at a time.
  • In and out points can be marked across clip boundaries as naturally as within a single clip, since the tape treats them as one continuous timeline.
  • Source Tape is only as useful as the bin it's built from — well-organized bins and smart bins make it dramatically more effective.
  • Source Tape solves fast browsing within one bin; reviewing multiple synced angles of the same moment is a different tool, covered next.
