Chapter Ten, Section Six

When to Use Fusion vs. When to Use Edit Page Tools

Part X of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 065 / 095
Depends On 10.5
Read Time ~7 Min

Every chapter in this part has, at some point, drawn a line between what the full Fusion page can do and what a lighter Edit page tool already handles — Chapter 10.1 pointed at Chapter 7.4's packaged effects, Chapter 10.4 flagged simpler stabilization tools directly. This closing chapter of Part X finally makes that recurring line the whole subject, gathering every version of it into one practical rule worth carrying forward.

The Escalation Ladder, Restated for the Whole Part

Chapter 7.3 built this idea first, specifically for titles: reach for the simplest tool that actually meets the need, and escalate only once it demonstrably doesn't. Text+ before templates, templates before Fusion titles. Part X has been the same ladder applied one level higher — the Edit page's packaged Fusion effects before a full Fusion composition, a built-in stabilizer before Fusion's tracker, a Text+ title before the node-by-node build Chapter 10.5 just walked through. The pattern repeats because it's the same underlying tradeoff every time: speed and simplicity on one end, complete control on the other, and the right choice is whichever one the shot actually requires, not whichever feels more capable.

Reading a Shot for Its Real Requirement

In practice, the tell is usually how many things a shot's effect actually needs to do at once, and whether they need to interact. A single, contained adjustment — a blur, a glow, a simple color tint on one element — is almost always a packaged Edit page effect's job, exactly as Chapter 7.4 described. A shot that needs a subject keyed, tracked onto a new background, and combined with a separately animated graphic — three operations that all depend on and interact with each other — is a full Fusion job by necessity, because no single packaged effect exposes controls for that many interacting pieces at once. The number of moving parts, and whether they genuinely need to talk to each other, is the honest test.

A packaged effect is a few Inspector sliders wrapped around one contained operation. Full Fusion is a graph, built for operations that need to see and depend on each other. The question is never which one is more powerful — it's which one the shot's actual complexity requires.

The Real Cost of Dropping Into Fusion

It's worth being honest about what opening the full Fusion page actually costs, beyond just the time spent building the composition. A Fusion composite is more computationally demanding to play back and render than a flattened clip or a packaged effect, which is a real, practical cost on longer or more complex timelines. It also introduces a genuinely separate environment to think in — node graphs, Merge nodes, trackers — pulling focus away from the Edit page's timeline rhythm for however long the composite takes to build. Neither cost makes Fusion the wrong choice when a shot genuinely needs it. Both costs are exactly why reaching for it by default, on shots that don't need it, is worth resisting.

A Concrete Decision, Worked Through

Take a lower-third with a logo that needs to sit convincingly on a slightly moving handheld shot. If the shot's motion is minor, a Text+ title with basic positioning, holding steady over gentle motion, is probably enough — no tracking required, no Fusion page opened. If the same shot has real, distracting camera movement that a static title would visibly slide against, that crosses into Chapter 10.4's tracking territory, and now Fusion is the honest answer, not because the title itself is more complex, but because it needs to be driven by real motion data a packaged effect has no way to provide. Same title, same intent — different tool, based entirely on what the specific shot demands.

Part X, Complete

That closes Part X. Between what compositing adds beyond a finished grade, Fusion's node-based structure and its Merge node, keying, tracking and stabilization, building a real title from the ground up, and now this closing discipline for knowing when any of it is actually warranted, the full arc of compositing work in Resolve is covered. The image itself — cut, graded, and now composited wherever it needed to be — is finished. What's left isn't creative work at all. Part XI turns to a different kind of problem entirely: getting that finished image correctly, reliably out of Resolve and into whatever form its actual destination requires, starting with what "delivering" a project even means.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Part X's tools follow the same escalation ladder Chapter 7.3 established for titles — simplest sufficient tool first, full Fusion only once genuinely needed.
  • The real test is how many operations a shot needs at once, and whether they need to interact — one contained effect versus several dependent ones.
  • Full Fusion compositions cost real playback and render performance, plus a genuine context switch — reasons to escalate deliberately, not by default.
  • The same title or effect can call for different tools on different shots — the deciding factor is always the specific shot's actual demands.
