Chapter Fifteen, Section Two

Sync Drift and Audio Problems

Part XV of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 088 / 095
Depends On 15.1
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 15.1 laid out a method: identify the category before reaching for a fix. This chapter applies it to one of the most common symptoms an editor ever encounters — audio that starts a scene in perfect sync with picture and is audibly behind or ahead of it by the time the scene ends. It's an unsettling problem precisely because nothing looks wrong at a glance; the desync only reveals itself over time, which is also the clue to what's actually causing it.

Drift Is Not the Same Problem as an Offset

It's worth separating two symptoms that look similar but have different causes. A constant offset — audio consistently a fixed number of frames early or late, from the very first frame to the last — is almost always a sync error at the source, the kind of misalignment the Sync Bin from Chapter 3.3 exists to correct. Drift is different: sync that starts accurate and gradually worsens over the course of a clip or timeline. A constant offset is a starting-point problem. Drift is a rate problem — something in the timeline is playing audio and picture at very slightly different speeds, and that tiny difference compounds the longer the clip runs.

Frame Rate Mismatches: The Usual Suspect

The single most common cause of true drift is a mismatch between a clip's actual frame rate and the project's timeline frame rate, particularly the subtle difference between a rate like 23.976 and its whole-number neighbor 24. Picture, timecode, and audio can all be nudged by that tiny percentage difference in ways that are invisible frame to frame but accumulate into a visible, audible gap over a multi-minute timeline. Chapter 2.7's case for setting project frame rate deliberately, rather than accepting a default, is directly about preventing exactly this outcome before it starts.

Variable Frame Rate Footage: The Sneaky Case

A second, less obvious cause is variable frame rate footage — common from phone cameras and some screen-recording tools — where the file's frame rate isn't actually constant despite claiming to be. Resolve, like most editing software, generally expects a constant rate, and variable-rate footage can drift in ways that don't trace back to any setting in the project at all, because the inconsistency lives inside the file itself. Footage from this kind of source is worth converting to a constant frame rate before it ever enters a timeline, rather than after drift has already appeared.

An offset is wrong from the first frame. Drift is accurate at the start and wrong by the end — and that distinction alone usually tells you whether you're looking at a sync error or a rate mismatch before you've changed a single setting.

Re-Establishing Sync With the Tools Already Covered

Once the cause is identified, re-syncing usually means returning to tools this book already introduced, now used diagnostically rather than for initial review. The Sync Bin's waveform-matching from Chapter 3.3, and the deeper timecode and waveform techniques from Chapter 6.2, both work as well for re-aligning a drifted clip as they do for an original multicam sync — the underlying problem, matching two sources against a shared reference point, is the same problem either way.

Linked Audio and Accidental Slips

Not every sync problem is a rate issue. Chapter 4.6 covered linking clips together so picture and audio move as one unit — and an accidentally unlinked clip, nudged during a trim, produces something that looks identical to drift without any frame rate mismatch behind it at all. This is exactly the kind of case Chapter 15.1's reproduction step catches quickly: if the desync is isolated to one clip rather than spreading across an entire timeline, a broken link is a far more likely cause than a rate mismatch.

Sync drift, in nearly every case, traces back to a rate mismatch, variable frame rate source material, or a broken link — not a mysterious, unfixable fault in the software. Chapter 15.3 turns from an audio problem to picture's own version of a broken connection: what happens when a project's clips lose their link to the media files behind them entirely.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • A constant offset is a starting-sync problem; drift that worsens over time is a rate mismatch — the two point to different fixes.
  • Frame rate mismatches, especially between neighboring rates like 23.976 and 24, are the most common cause of true drift.
  • Variable frame rate source footage can drift independent of any project setting, and is best converted to a constant rate before editing.
  • Drift isolated to a single clip, rather than an entire timeline, is more likely a broken link from Chapter 4.6 than a rate mismatch.
