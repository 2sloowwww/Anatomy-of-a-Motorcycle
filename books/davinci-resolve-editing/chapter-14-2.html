Chapter Fourteen, Section Two

Documentary Editing at Scale

Part XIV of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 083 / 095
Depends On 14.1
Read Time ~7 Min

Chapter 14.1 described narrative editing as choosing well among deliberately planned options. Documentary work shares almost none of that certainty. There is often no script, no shot list guaranteeing coverage of a given beat, and no guarantee the story you end up telling is the one you set out to shoot. What documentary editing does have, usually, is volume — hours of footage measured in the hundreds rather than the dozens — and that volume is the actual problem this chapter is about.

Volume Changes the Job Before Editing Even Starts

A scripted project's Media Pool is built around known scenes and known setups. A documentary project's Media Pool starts as an undifferentiated pile — weeks or months of interviews, verité footage, and B-roll, none of it pre-sorted into anything resembling a scene. Chapter 2.2's case for bins over folders and Chapter 2.4's Smart Bins matter here in a way they don't in scripted work: at documentary scale, organization isn't a convenience, it's the difference between a project that can be found again and one that can't.

Logging Is the Real First Cut

Long before a timeline exists, documentary footage gets logged — reviewed and tagged with keywords, subjects, and rough content notes, almost always through the metadata fields Chapter 2.4 introduced. This isn't busywork ahead of the real editing; it is a form of editing, since a well-logged project already contains, buried in its tags, most of the candidate material for a hundred different possible cuts. A Smart Bin built around a keyword like a specific interview subject or theme turns a search through hundreds of hours into a filtered list in seconds, which is the only way this volume of material stays workable at all.

Multicam for the Interview, Not Just the Concert

Documentary interviews are frequently shot on two or three cameras at once — a wide and a close angle, sometimes a cutaway camera on the interviewer — recording the exact same continuous, unrepeatable conversation simultaneously. That's precisely the situation Chapter 6.1 defined multicam footage around, and building a proper multicam clip for a documentary interview, rather than manually track-juggling between angles, pays off over the length a documentary interview usually runs. The live, playback-driven angle switching from Chapter 6.3 turns a forty-minute conversation into a usable rough cut in close to real time.

A scripted project is organized to protect a known structure. A documentary project is organized to make an unknown one findable — and at the scale documentary footage usually arrives in, that difference in purpose changes almost everything about how the Media Pool gets built.

The String-Out Before the Structure

Where scripted editing moves fairly directly from selects to assembly, documentary editing typically passes through an intermediate stage: the string-out, a rough sequential assembly of the strongest logged material, built before any real structural decisions are made. It exists to let an editor see what they actually have, in volume, before committing to an order — and it's normal, even expected, for a documentary's actual structure to emerge from this stage rather than precede it, which is close to the opposite of how a scripted scene gets approached.

Proxies Aren't Optional at This Scale

Chapter 2.5 covered proxies and optimized media as a general performance option. At documentary scale, with camera-original footage frequently running into terabytes across an entire shoot, they stop being optional and become close to a prerequisite for working at all — a system straining to play back full-resolution footage on every scrub through hundreds of hours of logged material loses far more time than proxy generation ever costs up front.

Documentary work trades the scripted world's certainty for volume, discovery, and a structure that often reveals itself only partway through the process. Chapter 14.3 moves to something close to documentary's opposite in scale and stakes — social media and vertical-format editing, where the unit of work shrinks to seconds rather than hours, and the format itself, not just the story, dictates how a cut gets built.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Documentary footage arrives as volume without pre-built structure — organization is the difference between findable and unusable, not just convenience.
  • Logging with metadata and Smart Bins from Chapter 2.4 functions as a real early stage of editing, not preparation separate from it.
  • Multi-camera documentary interviews are genuine multicam footage under Chapter 6.1's definition, and benefit from the same live-switching workflow.
  • A string-out precedes structure in documentary work, often revealing the actual shape of the piece rather than confirming a planned one.
