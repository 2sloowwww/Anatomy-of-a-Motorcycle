Chapter Twelve, Section Four

Remote Grading and Review Workflows

Part XII of XVI
Category Practical
Sheet 075 / 095
Depends On 12.3
Read Time ~6 Min

Chapter 12.3 closed on a distinction worth carrying forward: locking solves conflicts between people working inside the same room, on the same network, at roughly the same time. It says nothing about what happens once the team sharing a project isn't in the same building at all — a colorist grading from a home suite, a director reviewing from another city, a sound mixer in a studio three time zones away. The shared database from Chapter 12.2 doesn't stop working over distance, but distance introduces problems locking was never built to solve.

When the Team Isn't in the Same Room

A Project Server reachable over the internet, rather than only a local network, technically extends Collaboration Mode to anyone with a connection to it — the database mechanics from Chapter 12.2 don't inherently care whether a seat is down the hall or across an ocean. What changes is everything downstream of that connection: network latency stretches the "structured, periodic synchronization" Chapter 12.2 described into something slower and less predictable, and the media storage question that chapter raised — where the actual footage lives, separate from the project record itself — becomes considerably harder once "shared storage" can no longer mean a fast local server everyone in the building can read from directly.

Grading Remotely Without the Original Media Locally

This is exactly the situation where Chapter 2.5's proxies and optimized media stop being a convenience and become the thing that makes remote work possible at all. A remote colorist rarely has practical access to multi-terabyte camera originals sitting on a server in a different city; what actually gets sent, or streamed, is a lightweight proxy — small enough to transfer or access reasonably over a home internet connection, close enough in framing and exposure to grade against meaningfully. The grading decisions themselves, recorded as node-graph instructions rather than baked into pixels, apply identically once the timeline reconnects to full-resolution originals for final render, exactly the same non-destructive logic Chapter 2.5 described for editing carries over unchanged to remote grading.

Review Without Everyone in the Room

Client and director review has its own remote version, and it splits into two distinct modes worth telling apart. Asynchronous review uses tools already covered earlier in this book in a new context: a marker dropped on a timeline, with a note attached, as Chapter 4.5 described, becomes something more than a personal reminder once that timeline lives on a shared project. It becomes a message left directly for whoever opens that timeline next, wherever and whenever they happen to do it — feedback that doesn't require two people in the same room, or even the same day, to exchange. Synchronous remote review is the other mode: a live session where playback is watched together in real time, over a shared screen or a streamed feed, for the specific kind of feedback — pacing, a reaction watched live — that only really lands when it's witnessed happening, not read afterward as a note.

A marker with a note attached isn't just a personal reminder anymore. On a shared project, it's a message left directly on the timeline for whoever opens it next — asynchronous review that doesn't require everyone in the same room, or even the same day.

Bandwidth Is the New Bottleneck

It's worth naming plainly what remote collaboration actually trades one constraint for another. Working in the same room removes network distance as a factor but reintroduces every scheduling conflict of getting people physically together; working remotely solves the scheduling problem but makes bandwidth — for streamed review sessions, for proxy transfers, for the database's own synchronization traffic — the thing that now determines how smoothly the whole arrangement actually feels day to day. That's not a problem this chapter fully resolves; it's a thread that reappears directly in Part XIII, once storage and network move from being a collaboration concern to the book's central performance concern.

With locking handled inside a building and remote workflows handled across distance, Chapter 12.5 closes out Part XII by pulling both threads together — looking at how editorial, color, and sound teams actually coordinate across all of it on a real production, whether they're down the hall from each other or not.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Collaboration Mode extends over the internet, not just a local network — but distance stretches synchronization and complicates shared media access.
  • Remote grading typically works against proxies, not camera originals — grading decisions carry over to full-resolution media exactly as edits do.
  • Timeline markers with notes double as asynchronous review — feedback left for whoever opens the project next, no shared room required.
  • Remote work trades scheduling friction for bandwidth dependency — a constraint Part XIII returns to as a core performance concern.
