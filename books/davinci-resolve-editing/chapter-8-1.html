Audio Basics on the Edit Page

Part VIII of XVI

Sheet 044 / 095
Depends On 7.5
Read Time ~6 Min

Audio has been present in nearly every chapter of this book without ever being its subject. Chapter 3.3 synced it to picture in the Sync Bin. Chapter 4.6 linked it to video as a single selectable unit. Chapter 6.4 gave it a strategy inside multicam clips. In every case, sound was handled as a consequence of some other decision — never addressed on its own terms. Part VIII closes that gap, and it starts here, on the Edit page, before the book ever opens the dedicated Fairlight environment two chapters from now.

Audio Tracks Are Not Silent Video Tracks

Chapter 4.2 described tracks and stacking order in mostly visual terms — which video track draws on top of which. Audio tracks follow the same stacking logic Chapter 4.2 introduced, arranged below the video tracks in the same timeline, but what fills them looks nothing like a video clip's thumbnail strip. An audio clip on the Edit page displays as a waveform: a horizontal trace of amplitude over time, tall where the sound is loud, flat where it's quiet or silent. Learning to read that trace at a glance is the first real audio skill this book asks of you, and it pays off immediately, because a waveform tells you things a video thumbnail never could — exactly where a word starts, exactly where a room goes quiet, exactly where a hard sound effect lands.

Reading a Waveform Before You Ever Touch a Fader

Long before loudness or mixing enters the picture, a waveform is a navigation tool. A tight cluster of tall peaks separated by narrow gaps usually reads as speech — syllables and breaths. A long, low, even hum usually reads as room tone or ambient noise. A sudden, sharp spike against an otherwise flat trace usually reads as a hard effect — a door, a footstep, a hit. None of this requires playback to notice; it's visible directly in the shape of the trace, which is exactly why experienced editors cut dialogue by eye first and confirm by ear second, using the waveform to find the edit point before JKL, from Chapter 5.2, refines it.

A waveform isn't decoration on top of an audio clip — it's the fastest way to find an edit point without listening to the whole clip first. Reading it well is a navigation skill before it's ever a mixing skill.

Clip Gain: The Level Overlay Line

Every audio clip on the timeline carries a thin horizontal line running across its waveform — the clip's gain overlay. Dragging that line up or down raises or lowers the clip's level directly, and dragging a point on the line creates a keyframe, using the exact same playhead-and-value pattern Chapter 7.5 described for every other keyframeable property in Resolve. This is a genuinely useful tool for quick, local fixes: one line of dialogue recorded a touch too quiet, one sound effect that needs to duck under a word. It is deliberately not the tool for building a full mix. The level overlay adjusts one clip in isolation; it has no concept of busses, sends, or how that clip's level relates to everything else playing under it — which is precisely the gap Fairlight exists to fill, starting in Chapter 8.2.

Linking Audio and Video, and When to Break It

Chapter 4.6 covered linking in general terms — clips joined so that selecting or moving one selects or moves the other. Audio and video are the most common linked pair in any real project, kept together automatically so a cut never accidentally separates a line of dialogue from the face saying it. That link is a default, not a rule, and it's routinely broken on purpose: sliding a sound effect a few frames early for impact, extending a music cue past its picture cut, or replacing a clip's audio entirely without touching its video. Unlinking, adjusting, and relinking is a normal, frequent part of audio work on the Edit page, not a workaround for a mistake.

Monitoring: What You're Actually Hearing

One detail is easy to overlook and worth naming plainly before this chapter closes: what you hear while editing depends heavily on what you're hearing it through. Laptop speakers, consumer headphones, and a calibrated studio monitor can each make the same clip sound meaningfully different — thinner, boomier, or missing frequencies entirely — and a level or an EQ decision that seems obviously correct on one can seem obviously wrong on another. The on-screen audio meters, tracking level in decibels regardless of what's plugged in, are the one consistent reference in that chain, and getting comfortable reading them now is worth more than it might seem, because Chapter 8.4 builds an entire chapter on exactly what those numbers need to mean before delivery.

Everything in this chapter — waveforms, clip gain, linking, meters — lives entirely inside the Edit page, using the same timeline every earlier part of this book has already made familiar. Chapter 8.2 explains why that's not actually where serious audio work happens in Resolve, and what a fully separate page, built as a complete digital audio workstation rather than a timeline feature, adds on top of everything covered here.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Audio tracks share the Edit page's stacking logic from Chapter 4.2, but display waveforms instead of thumbnails — a shape worth learning to read on sight.
  • A clip's gain overlay line, adjustable and keyframeable, fixes one clip's level in isolation — it isn't a mixing tool.
  • Audio-video linking from Chapter 4.6 is a default, not a rule — unlinking to shift or replace one side is routine, not a workaround.
  • On-screen meters, unlike speakers or headphones, are the one consistent reference for level regardless of monitoring setup.
