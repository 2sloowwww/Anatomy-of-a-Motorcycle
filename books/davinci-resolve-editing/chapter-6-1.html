Chapter Six, Section One

What Multicam Editing Solves

Part VI of XVI
Category Foundational
Sheet 034 / 095
Depends On 3.3
Read Time ~7 Min

A sit-down interview covered by two cameras. A wedding ceremony shot from three angles at once. A panel discussion, a concert, a podcast recorded on four synced feeds. Chapter 3.3 already named what these situations share: multiple sources capturing the exact same moment simultaneously. The Sync Bin solved half of that problem — reviewing those sources together. This chapter opens Part VI by addressing the other half: what happens once reviewing is done and you actually need to build a timeline that switches between those angles, cut by cut, the way a live broadcast does.

What Actually Counts as Multicam Footage

Not every project with more than one camera is a multicam project in the sense this part means. A narrative scene covered with an A-camera and a B-camera, where each setup is later chosen from selectively for specific shots, is still fundamentally single-camera thinking applied twice — you pick the better angle for a given moment and cut away from the other entirely. Multicam footage is narrower and more specific: multiple cameras recording the same continuous, unrepeatable action at the same time, where the edit's job is to move fluidly between all of them as that one take unfolds, not to select once and discard the rest.

Why Manual Cutting Doesn't Scale Here

Every tool covered so far is, in principle, capable of building this kind of cut by hand: placing each angle on its own track as Chapter 4.2 described, aligning them using the same timecode or waveform logic Chapter 3.3 introduced, and then manually trimming and deleting across tracks to switch which angle is audible and visible at a given moment. This works, technically, for a handful of switches. It stops working as a practical method the moment the material demands what real multicam footage usually demands — a forty-minute interview might call for two hundred angle changes, each one requiring the same manual track-juggling, repeated hundreds of times over.

Nothing covered so far is incapable of building a multicam-style cut. What none of it offers is speed at the scale real multicam material demands, where a single interview might call for hundreds of angle switches rather than a handful.

From Reviewing to Cutting: Why the Sync Bin Isn't Enough

Chapter 3.3 closed on exactly this distinction: the Sync Bin is the tool for looking and deciding, not for committing to a cut. It aligns sources in time so you can compare them, but it deliberately stops short of merging them into anything new — the clips it aligns remain separate, individual items in the Media Pool, exactly as they were before. That's the right behavior for the reviewing stage, where you don't yet know whether formal multicam structure is even worth the setup. It's the wrong behavior once you do know, because reviewing alignment and editing alignment are different problems, and the Sync Bin was only ever built to solve the first one.

What a Multicam Clip Actually Is

A multicam clip is a new kind of object, distinct from either an ordinary clip or a Sync Bin grouping. It bundles several synced angle clips into a single item that lives in the Media Pool as one entry rather than several, carrying a switchable angle property as part of what defines it. Placed on a timeline, a multicam clip still behaves like one clip — one bar, one set of edit points — but which angle is actually being shown, and optionally which angle's audio is being heard, can be changed at any point along its length without disturbing the clip's position or duration. This is a heavier commitment than the Sync Bin's temporary alignment, and it's meant to be: you build a multicam clip once you already know you're cutting between these angles, not while you're still deciding whether to.

Editing Live, Not After the Fact

The real payoff of that heavier structure shows up in how a multicam clip is actually cut. Rather than manually placing and trimming across separate tracks, a multicam clip can be played back in a dedicated viewer showing all its angles at once, with angle switches made in real time during playback — a keypress or a click on the active angle while the material is running. The effect is closer to directing a live broadcast than to conventional editing: you watch the material unfold and choose angles to the rhythm of what's happening, and those choices become edit points automatically, rather than being constructed afterward one at a time. This is the categorical difference Part VI is built around — not a faster version of manual track-switching, but a genuinely different way of making the cutting decision in the first place.

The remainder of this part builds toward that live-cutting workflow in order. Chapter 6.2 returns to timecode and waveform sync in more technical depth, now in service of actually constructing a multicam clip rather than just a temporary review alignment. Chapter 6.3 covers the real-time cutting process this chapter has only described. Chapters 6.4 and 6.5 close out the part with multicam-specific audio strategy and Group Clips, a lighter alternative worth knowing about even after multicam itself is second nature.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Multicam footage means multiple synced cameras capturing the same continuous action at once — not separately shot coverage chosen from later.
  • Manual, track-by-track cutting works in principle but doesn't scale to the number of angle switches real multicam material demands.
  • A multicam clip bundles several angles into one Media Pool object with a switchable angle property — a commitment beyond the Sync Bin's temporary alignment.
  • A multicam clip lets you switch angles live during playback, turning the cutting decision into something closer to directing than to manual construction.
