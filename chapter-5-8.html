Compression and Rebound Adjustment

Part V of XVI
Category Practical / Engineering
Sheet 052 / 150
Depends On Ch. 5.7
Read Time ~8 Min

"High-speed" and "low-speed" damping have nothing to do with how fast the motorcycle is travelling down the road — a distinction that trips up more riders than almost any other piece of suspension vocabulary. Both terms describe how fast the suspension shaft itself is moving, and getting that straight is the key to understanding why some suspension units offer far more adjustment than a single compression knob and a single rebound knob.

Chapter 5.6 and Chapter 5.7 covered how forks and shocks are physically built. This chapter covers how a rider or mechanic actually adjusts the compression and rebound damping Chapter 5.3 introduced, on that real hardware.

Clickers: Simple Mechanisms, Real Range

Most adjustable forks and shocks provide separate adjusters, often called clickers for the audible detents they turn through, for compression and rebound damping independently. Physically, these usually work by opening or closing a needle valve that restricts internal oil flow more or less — turning an adjuster to increase damping narrows that restriction, requiring more force to push oil through at a given speed; turning it the other way opens the restriction, letting oil flow more freely and reducing damping force. It's a simple mechanism, but it provides real, meaningful adjustment range within the damper's designed limits, without requiring the internal rebuild that changing the fundamental valving would need.

High-Speed and Low-Speed Damping: Shaft Speed, Not Road Speed

On more sophisticated suspension, compression damping in particular is sometimes split further into separate low-speed and high-speed adjusters — and the "speed" in both terms refers entirely to how fast the suspension shaft is moving in and out, not the motorcycle's road speed. Low-speed compression damping governs gradual, relatively slow shaft movement: braking dive, cornering load, small ripples the suspension absorbs smoothly and continuously. High-speed compression damping governs sudden, sharp shaft movement: hitting a pothole or a square-edged bump, where the suspension has to react almost instantly to a single severe input.

These two situations genuinely benefit from different damping characteristics, which is exactly why separating them matters. A setting that feels plush and well-controlled for the slow, gradual inputs low-speed damping handles can still let the suspension crash harshly through its travel on a sharp single hit if high-speed damping isn't controlled separately — and the reverse mismatch, firm high-speed control paired with harsh low-speed damping, produces a suspension that handles sharp hits well but feels needlessly stiff over everyday small bumps. This is a direct, practical consequence of the velocity-based physics Chapter 5.3 described: since damping force depends on how fast the shaft is moving, and shaft speed varies enormously between a gentle brake dive and a sudden pothole strike, treating all speeds identically was always going to be a compromise.

High-speed and low-speed refer to how fast the suspension itself is moving, never to how fast the motorcycle is going. A slow-speed parking lot pothole can demand high-speed compression damping, and a fast highway sweeper with a smooth surface may never ask for it at all.

Adjusting Methodically

Suspension adjustment rewards a deliberate, methodical approach rather than large, hopeful changes. Before touching anything, it's worth noting the factory or current baseline setting, usually counted in clicks from fully closed, so there's always a known reference point to return to. From there, changing one adjuster at a time, in small increments, and actually testing the result before making another change is the only way to know which adjustment produced which effect — changing compression and rebound together, or making large jumps, makes it far harder to tell what actually happened and why.

A Common Misconception, Cleared Up Early

When a suspension feels wrong — harsh, wallowy, unsettled — it's tempting to reach straight for the damping clickers, in either direction, as the default fix. Chapter 5.2 already closed with the warning that spring rate cannot be judged in isolation from damping; the same warning runs the other way here. If sag, covered in Chapter 5.4, is badly out of range, or the spring rate itself is mismatched to the rider's weight, no amount of compression or rebound adjustment can substitute for fixing that underlying cause — damping clickers have a limited range, exactly like preload does, and they cannot compensate for a fundamentally wrong spring any more than preload alone could in Chapter 5.4's discussion. Diagnosing a suspension problem correctly means checking sag and spring rate first, and only reaching for the damping adjusters once those are already confirmed correct.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Compression and rebound clickers typically work by restricting internal oil flow through a needle valve, providing real adjustment range within a damper's designed limits.
  • High-speed and low-speed damping refer to how fast the suspension shaft itself is moving, not the motorcycle's road speed — a distinction worth getting right before adjusting anything.
  • Low-speed compression handles gradual inputs like braking dive and cornering load; high-speed compression handles sudden, sharp inputs like potholes — the two can require genuinely different settings.
  • Adjusting suspension methodically — noting a baseline, changing one setting at a time, testing before changing again — is the only reliable way to know which adjustment produced which effect.
  • Damping adjustment cannot fix a fundamentally wrong spring rate or badly set sag — those causes need to be confirmed correct first, exactly as preload alone cannot fix a mismatched spring in Chapter 5.4.
